AI for Quality Control Documentation in Construction

18 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

The pour is booked for Wednesday. The reinforcement is fixed, the formwork is closed, the concrete is ordered, and the inspection and test plan puts a hold point between the steel being complete and the first load arriving. The engineer walked the bay yesterday and photographed it. What nobody can produce this morning is evidence that the client's representative was invited with the notice the contract specifies, or the signed release for the adjacent bay. The trade is standing there with a full crew, and somebody senior is deciding whether to pour on a phone call and write the paperwork afterwards.

Why covered work is a different documentation problem

A factory can re-test. A laboratory can pull the retain sample. On site, once concrete has gone in or a ceiling has closed, the work underneath is gone. The evidence that it was correct either exists as a record made at the time, in the right place, with the right things attached, or it does not exist at all. Recreating it is not expensive. It is impossible.

That reorders the priorities. In most quality systems a missing record costs the effort of producing it late. Here it costs destructive investigation, or an argument at handover you lose on paper however well the thing was built. So the question to ask of an agent is not whether it reviews documents faster. It is whether it can tell you what evidence is missing while the evidence can still be captured.

The inspection and test plan as a live sequence

Most inspection and test plans are written at tender, approved once, and then live as documents rather than as a state. The plan says what applies to a type of work. What the site needs is narrower and changes daily: for this bay, on this level, in this package, which hold and witness points apply, which are released, and which prior records must exist before a release can be requested.

An agent suits that, because the plan is a rule set and the site is a stream of events. It expands the approved plans against the location schedule and reconciles them with the short term programme, producing a list per package: hold points the programme reaches shortly with no notification yet, points notified where a prerequisite record is absent, and points released on paper while the referenced inspection record is blank. It should also flag the plan that no longer matches the work, because when a design change alters the sequence the plan rarely follows.

The notification chain before an inspection

An inspection held without the right people invited, in the right form, with the notice the contract requires, may not count.

An agent can run the chain. It knows from the contract and the package who must be invited for each hold and witness point, what notice they are owed and in what form. It drafts the notification with the location, the activity, the plan reference and the proposed time, for the person authorised to issue it. It tracks acknowledgement, and when the clock runs out it drafts the record the contract's own mechanism depends on: notice given, attendance not forthcoming, work proceeding by the route the contract provides. Written on the day with the correspondence attached, that record is worth a great deal at final account. Written from memory months later it is worth very little.

Turning a phone full of photographs into a record

The real capture event on most sites is a person with a phone taking pictures and typing a few words. Everything that makes those pictures into evidence, the location, the level, the grid reference, the package, the plan item, the date, the signature, gets added later by somebody who was not there, or never gets added at all.

An agent narrows that gap. From what was captured and what the engineer was scheduled to inspect, it drafts the record: the probable activity and plan item, the package, the sequence of the images, and the fields it can populate from systems that already hold them. Then it asks the questions it cannot infer, at a moment when the answer takes seconds. Crucially it proposes the location and asks for confirmation. It never asserts one.

Nonconformance reports and the ones that never close

Raising a nonconformance against a subcontractor still working on your site is a social act as much as a technical one, which is part of why so many are raised late, written thinly and left open. The report gets issued, a disposition is discussed in a meeting, the work moves on, and the record sits with no closure evidence until somebody assembling the handover file finds it.

Two things here are agent work. The first is drafting: taking the engineer's observation, attaching the specification clause it departs from, pulling the affected locations and the relevant delivery or inspection records, and stating the condition without pre-empting the disposition. The second is closeout, where the value sits. An ageing report telling you a nonconformance is old tells you nothing. An agent that has read the record can name what it waits on: a disposition sitting with a named person, a proposal accepted but never executed, or work rectified with no photograph, no re-inspection and no signature to show for it. That last category is the one that reappears at handover.

Material traceability before the material is covered

Delivery tickets, batch and mill records, product data and test information arrive in a rush at a gate, get signed by whoever is nearest, and are matched against the specification much later, if at all.

An agent reading the ticket at delivery can check while the check still has value: does what arrived match the order, does the order match the specification, and is the supporting documentation attached. It then holds the link nobody records and everybody eventually needs, which is which delivery went into which part of the works.

Chasing the subcontractor quality package

A package is not complete until several companies have each sent something, and each is optimising for its own payment date rather than for the evidence trail your client contract requires.

An agent maintains the required document matrix per package and per company, and chases by named document: this certificate, for this equipment tag, in this location, outstanding since this date. It reads what arrives, checks the obvious fields against the schedule, files it, and reports completeness per company rather than for the project as a whole. The leverage that makes people respond stays commercial and human. The agent supplies the specificity that makes the request unarguable.

Commissioning records, witnessed against issued

At the end of a package there is a meaningful difference between a test that was witnessed and a certificate that was issued. A certificate asserts a result. A witnessed record shows who was present, what procedure was followed, what readings were taken, and against which equipment identifier.

An agent checking commissioning documentation looks for that distinction: results recorded rather than a pass declared, the identifier matched to the equipment schedule, the procedure referenced and the witness named. Certificates that assert without evidencing get flagged while the commissioning team is still on site and can repeat the test.

Handover completeness and the release of retention

The handover file is an assembly problem at a scale people underestimate: thousands of documents, from dozens of parties, indexed against a structure the client specified. Its completeness is what stands between your team and the release of retention money, which makes quality documentation the last commercial gate on the job rather than an administrative tail.

Treated as an end of job exercise it fails predictably. Companies have demobilised, the people who witnessed the tests have moved on, defects closed months ago have no closure evidence, and the client rejects submissions for reasons that were fixable while the work was live. Treated as a continuous state it behaves differently. From the first package the agent holds the client's required index, maps every expected document to the party that owes it, files what arrives, and reports what is outstanding by name. Snag and defect closures meet the same standard as inspection records, which is that a closure without evidence is not a closure.

Where AI fails here

The agent cannot look at the work. It has no view of the reinforcement, the fixings or the membrane, and nothing it produces is an inspection. It reads records, checks them against rules, chases what is absent and drafts what can be drafted from what a person captured.

It cannot tell whether a photograph shows the location it claims. This is the sharpest limit in the use case. The system can propose a location from context, but confirmation is a human act and has to stay one, because a record that looks complete and documents the wrong bay is materially worse than a visible gap. A gap gets investigated. A confident wrong record gets relied upon.

It does not replace the inspector's judgement and it certifies nothing. Whether a surface is acceptable, whether a defect is cosmetic or structural, whether a nonconformance should be accepted or rejected, and whether a package is fit to sign off belong to named people with the competence and the liability to decide.

It is also only as good as its baseline. If the plans in the system are superseded, the agent will chase the wrong documents with great persistence. Handwritten and photographed records extract less reliably than clean digital input, and the dangerous error is not the unreadable field but the misread one that looks plausible. Configure for escalation over guessing, and measure accuracy on your own site records rather than a demonstration set.

Where to start

Start with a package you have already handed over, including the submissions the client rejected. Ground truth exists, nothing is at risk, and you get two honest numbers: what the agent finds that your team did not, and what your team found that the agent missed. The rejected items matter most, because they tell you what your client treats as incomplete.

From there the contained deployments are the inspection and test plan state against the short term programme, and the subcontractor document chase. Neither touches anybody's judgement, both are measurable within weeks, and both put pressure on evidence that can still be captured. Nonconformance closeout follows once the records the agent depends on are trustworthy.
