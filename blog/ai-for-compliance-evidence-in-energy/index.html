AI for Compliance Evidence in Energy: Proving What Happened at the Asset

16 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Compliance evidence in a business that owns physical infrastructure is not created in a room you control. It is created outdoors, in a trench or at a substation gate in the rain, by a crew whose signal dropped an hour ago. It arrives as a form filled in from a truck cab, a photograph of a nameplate, shorthand in a logbook, a certificate emailed by a contractor.

Then somebody asks a precise question about one asset. Show me its inspection history, the permit in force when that work was done, the competency of the crew, the calibration of the instrument. The answer almost always exists somewhere. The cost is finding it, proving it belongs to that asset and not a similar one, and being honest about what is missing.

Where the cost actually sits

The expensive thing is not judgement. It is reconciliation across a dispersed estate, where generation sites, feeders, pipelines, substations, wind and solar assets sit in a register that is rarely perfectly right, and the evidence about them lives in work management systems, document stores, handheld apps, contractor portals, shared drives and paper archives, none of it designed to answer a question about one asset.

So the trail gets assembled by hand, under pressure, by the few people who know where things are kept. Retrieval across messy sources, reconciliation against a register and flagging what does not line up is exactly what agents are good at. The decision stays with a named person.

Inspection and maintenance records tied to the right asset

An inspection is worthless as evidence if it is filed against the wrong asset. That happens constantly on dispersed networks, where identifiers are similar, plant has been replaced but kept its predecessor's record, and the crew described the location in local language.

An agent reads the completed record alongside the work order, the register entry, the location data captured by the device and any photographs, then decides whether the asset identity asserted in the record is supported by the rest of the evidence. Where identity is contested it does not quietly pick the likeliest candidate. It marks the record unresolved, names the competing assets and routes it to whoever owns that part of the estate.

Permits to work, isolation and switching records

Permit and isolation paperwork is high consequence and often the least tidy, because it is produced at the point of work by people whose attention is on the work. Sheets are scanned late, cancellations are recorded on a different system from issues, and the switching log lives with the control room.

An agent assembles the trail around a job: issue, the isolation applied, the switching that created it, the people named, any extension and the return to service. It checks that chain for internal consistency, whether the work described matches what was authorised, and whether every permit has a recorded cancellation. Breaks go to the responsible officer as a description of what is missing.

Contractor competency, induction and site access

Most work on a large estate is done by people who do not work for you. Their competency records, tickets, clearances and inductions sit in their systems, your access control system, a portal, or a folder somebody maintains out of goodwill.

An agent keeps a live view per organisation and per individual: authorisations held, expiry dates, inductions completed for which sites, and the work each person actually performed. The valuable output is the cross-reference nobody does by hand: a task carried out by someone whose authorisation had expired, had not been issued, or never covered that work.

Calibration certificates and the instrument behind a reading

A test result is only evidence if the instrument that produced it was in calibration on the day. In practice the reading sits in one system and the certificate in another, joined only retrospectively, when challenged.

An agent links each recorded test to the instrument serial the crew entered, retrieves the certificate covering that date, and confirms it spans the measurement and the range used. It flags readings taken with lapsed instruments, instruments in use with no certificate on file, and certificates expiring before scheduled campaigns, each flag carrying the certificate reference so a reviewer verifies rather than trusts.

Environmental monitoring and incident notification

Environmental evidence is periodic, tied to location, and often collected by third parties. Sampling results, discharge and emissions records, noise and waste documentation arrive on their own schedules and formats, and the duty to notify an event runs on a clock that starts before anyone has the facts.

An agent reconciles what was collected against what your monitoring programme required at each location, flagging gaps while there is still time to act. When an event occurs it assembles the pack: asset and location, operational data around the window, monitoring results, and the relevant parts of whatever reporting framework your market uses. It drafts. A named person decides whether an event is notifiable and signs it.

Defect notices and the remediation trail behind them

A defect raised and never closed is the most common finding on a physical estate. The second most common is a defect closed with an assertion rather than evidence.

An agent tracks every defect from the inspection that raised it through its risk assessment, the remedial order, the completion evidence and the verification that it is gone. It reports two failure shapes: defects open beyond the interval your own procedure sets for their severity, and defects closed with no artefact behind the closure. Safety observations and near miss reports run on the same machinery, where the useful question is not how many were raised but how many produced an action with evidence.

Land, access and consent agreements

Asset compliance is not only about steel and copper. Access rights, wayleaves, consents and landowner agreements govern whether you can lawfully be there, and they expire and transfer with land sales. An agent keeps the link between an agreement, the assets it covers and the work done under it, surfacing assets with no traceable access right and agreements expiring with scheduled work behind them. Interpretation stays with your property and legal teams.

Connecting a photograph to the asset it actually shows

Field photographs are the richest evidence you hold and the least usable, because they are stored against jobs rather than assets.

An agent reads the image alongside its capture time and location, the job it was attached to and the register entry, then proposes the link. Where a nameplate, serial or label is legible, that is strong evidence and the agent should cite the visible text. Where the link rests only on proximity, that is weak evidence and must be labelled weak. A photograph of plausible plant that is not the right plant is worse than none, because it looks like proof.

The evidence pack when an inspector arrives and asks

The timed exercise is the real test. Somebody names one asset and follows the thread from the inspection to the defect it raised, the permit the repair ran under, the competency of the crew and the sign off.

An agent resolves that chain into specific documents, retrieves them, checks each for completeness and legibility, and produces an indexed pack stating explicitly what was not found and why. That last part matters most: a pack which quietly omits what is missing is a liability. The same checks run between inspections, so gaps surface on your timetable rather than in front of a visitor.

Evaluating it against your own history

Do not evaluate this on a demo. Build the test set from evidence whose answer you already know.

Take audits you have been through, where the questions asked and the packs produced are on record, and have the agent assemble those packs from scratch. Compare in both directions: what it missed, and what it found that the manual effort did not. Then take asset files your engineers consider genuinely good and files they consider poor, and check the agent rates them as your engineers do. An agent that calls a thin file complete has failed the only test that counts.

Where AI fails here

Failures rooted in the record

If the asset register is wrong, the agent files good evidence confidently against the wrong asset, at scale, with a tidy citation trail. Neatness is persuasive. Register quality is the ceiling on everything above, which is why identity resolution has to be allowed to return unresolved.

Photographs mislead. An image can show something correct in type and entirely plausible while being the wrong unit, and a model that wants to be helpful will match it anyway. Field notes carry shorthand and local names only the crew understands, and interpreting that shorthand is where a confident wrong reading is most likely.

Failures rooted in how it is deployed

The worst outcome here is an agent smoothing over a genuine gap. If a record does not exist, the honest output is that it does not exist, not an inference about what probably happened or an adjacent record offered as the missing one. That manufactures false assurance in a safety context.

Resist letting the agent decide compliance. It assembles evidence and flags conflicts. Whether the estate is compliant, whether an event is notifiable, whether an asset stays in service: those are judgements a named person makes and signs.

And a tidy evidence pack over a poor maintenance culture is worse than an untidy honest one, because it makes the real problem harder to see. If early output shows the work is not being done, that is the finding, not a paperwork problem to automate.

Where to start

Start where the agent only reads and the ground truth is checkable. Calibration linkage, authorisation expiry and open defect ageing qualify: structured inputs, an obvious owner, and an error that costs a conversation rather than harm.

Pick one asset class at one site rather than the whole estate. Run the agent alongside the current process and keep the comparison, which is both your accuracy evidence and your answer on whether to widen scope. Bring the people who face your regulator into the design early, because if they will not accept the trail the agent produces, the work has no value.
