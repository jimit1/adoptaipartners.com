AI for Invoice Processing: A Practical Guide

If you run a finance or operations team, you already know where the hours go. Invoices arrive as PDF attachments, scanned images, portal downloads and the occasional photographed paper copy. Someone keys them into the ERP. Someone else chases the approver who is on leave. A third person untangles the mismatch between the invoice, the purchase order and the goods receipt, usually two days before month end. This work consumes skilled people, delays close, strains supplier relationships and occasionally lets a duplicate or fraudulent invoice slip through.

AI agents change the economics of this work, but only when they are built properly: connected to your actual systems of record, given narrow and auditable authority, and designed to hand off to a human at the right moments. This guide walks through the concrete use cases, how each one works in practice, and where the technology honestly falls short.

Capture and extraction that actually reads the invoice

The first job is turning an unstructured document into structured data. Traditional OCR templates break every time a supplier redesigns their invoice layout. A modern extraction agent reads the document the way a person does: it finds the supplier name, invoice number, dates, line items, tax treatment and payment terms regardless of where they sit on the page.

How it works: the agent monitors your AP inbox or supplier portal, reads each document, and classifies it first. Is this an invoice, a credit note, a reminder, or a statement? It then extracts the fields your ERP requires, validates them against what it already knows (does this supplier exist in the vendor master, does the VAT number match, do the line totals sum to the invoice total), and writes a draft invoice record into the ERP with the source document attached.

When it hands to a human: any invoice from an unrecognised supplier, any document where extracted totals fail arithmetic checks, and any document the agent cannot confidently classify. These land in a review queue with the agent's best guess pre-filled, so the human corrects rather than re-keys.

Two-way and three-way matching

Matching is where most AP time disappears. The invoice says one thing, the purchase order says another, and the goods receipt says a third. Most mismatches are boring: a unit price that moved by a few pence, or a partial delivery.

How it works: the agent reads the draft invoice, retrieves the related purchase order and receipt records from the ERP, and compares them line by line. It recognises common patterns that a rigid rules engine misses, such as an invoice line that covers two PO lines, a delivery charge that appears on the invoice but not the PO, or a supplier who invoices in a different unit of measure. Where everything matches within your tolerance policy, the agent marks the invoice matched and posts it for payment scheduling. Where there is a small, explainable variance, it documents the reasoning on the invoice record so an approver sees the explanation, not just a red flag.

When it hands to a human: variances above tolerance, invoices with no findable PO, and any case where accepting the variance would change what your organisation pays. The agent never resolves a price dispute on its own. It prepares the comparison, drafts the query to the supplier if you want one, and waits.

Coding non-PO invoices to the general ledger

Utilities, subscriptions, professional fees and rent rarely have purchase orders, so someone has to decide the GL account, cost centre and tax treatment for each one. This is repetitive judgement work.

How it works: the agent reads the invoice content and the supplier's history in your ledger. If the last twelve invoices from this software vendor were coded to the same account and cost centre, and this invoice looks like the others, the agent applies the same coding and notes its reasoning. It also reads context a simple rule cannot: an invoice from a law firm that references a property transaction should be coded differently from one referencing employment advice, and the agent can see that distinction in the line descriptions.

What it writes back: the coded invoice in the ERP, with a confidence note and the precedent it relied on, so your controllers can audit the logic months later.

When it hands to a human: first invoices from new suppliers, invoices that look materially different from the supplier's history, anything touching capitalisation decisions, and any coding the agent rates as low confidence. Over time the review queue shrinks because every human correction becomes precedent the agent reads next time.

Approval routing and the chasing nobody wants to do

Approval delay, not data entry, is often why invoices are paid late. The invoice sits in someone's inbox while early payment discounts expire and suppliers start calling.

How it works: the agent reads your delegation of authority policy and the invoice details, then routes each invoice to the correct approver based on amount, cost centre and category. It watches the queue. If an approver has not acted within your defined window, it sends a reminder with the context needed to decide quickly: what the invoice is for, whether it matched, what was paid last time. If the approver is out of office, it routes to the named delegate under the same policy rather than letting the invoice stall.

What it writes back: a complete approval trail in the system of record, including every reminder and reroute, which is precisely what your auditors ask for.

When it hands to a human: it never approves anything itself. Approval remains a human act. The agent's job is to make sure the right human sees the right invoice with the right context at the right time.

Duplicate and anomaly detection

Duplicate payments are embarrassing and common, because the same invoice arrives twice through different channels with slightly different formatting. Fraudulent invoices are rarer but more expensive.

How it works: before an invoice is posted, the agent compares it against recent history using more than exact field matching. It recognises that invoice INV-0234 and INV0234 from the same supplier for the same amount a week apart are probably the same document, and that a sudden change in a supplier's bank details deserves attention even when everything else looks normal. It also flags pattern anomalies: an invoice far outside a supplier's usual amount range, a new supplier whose details resemble an existing one, or round-number invoices arriving just under an approval threshold.

When it hands to a human: every flag. The agent blocks the invoice from the payment run and presents its evidence. Releasing a flagged invoice, and especially accepting changed bank details, is always a human decision, ideally with a call-back control the agent can prompt but never perform.

Supplier queries and statement reconciliation

A meaningful share of AP inbox traffic is suppliers asking when they will be paid, sending statements, or disputing a short payment.

How it works: the agent reads the incoming message, identifies the supplier and the invoices in question, and looks up their real status in the ERP: received, matched, approved, scheduled, paid, and the expected payment date. It drafts a specific reply from that data and places it in a queue for your team to review and send. For statements, it reconciles every line against your ledger and produces a short exception list: invoices the supplier claims you have that you cannot find, and credits you hold that they have not applied.

When it hands to a human: every outbound reply is reviewed before it goes anywhere, and any dispute over amounts or terms goes straight to a person with the full history attached.

Where to start

Resist the temptation to automate the whole flow at once. The pattern that works:

Start with one document type and one hand-off. Capture and extraction into a review queue is the usual first step, because a human checks every invoice, so risk during the learning period is close to zero while the time saving is immediate.

Measure the baseline first. Know your current cost per invoice, first-time match rate, and average days from receipt to approval. Without these you cannot tell whether the agent is earning its keep.

Keep the ERP as the single source of truth. The agent should read from and write to your existing systems, not maintain a parallel database that drifts out of sync.

Widen authority gradually. Once the review queue shows the agent is right on a category with high consistency, let that category post straight through and keep humans on the exceptions. Expansion should follow evidence, not enthusiasm.

Involve your auditors early. An agent that logs its reasoning on every record is easier to audit than the manual process it replaced, but only if you design the trail before go-live rather than after.

Where AI fails here

Honesty matters more than optimism in finance automation, so here is where this technology genuinely struggles.

Terrible source documents are still terrible. A blurry photograph of a crumpled invoice will produce low-confidence extraction, and the correct behaviour is to route it to a human, not to guess.

Judgement without precedent. The agent codes a novel invoice type badly because there is nothing to reason from. First occurrences belong with people.

Policy that lives in people's heads. If your tolerance rules, delegation matrix and coding conventions are not written down anywhere, the agent cannot follow them. Many deployments stall here, and the fix is documentation work, not model work.

Confidently wrong output. Language models can produce a plausible answer that is simply incorrect. This is why well-built agents carry confidence thresholds, validation checks against the ledger, and mandatory human review on anything that moves money or changes supplier details. Any vendor promising full autonomy on day one is describing a system you should not buy.

Organisational change. The technology is often the easy part. Redefining roles, retraining the team from keying to reviewing, and earning your controllers' trust takes deliberate effort that no software ships with.

None of this argues against automating invoice processing. It argues for doing it in stages, with clear hand-off points and evidence at each step, which is exactly how the successful deployments we see are run.
