AI for RFP Responses in Consumer Goods: Range Reviews, Product Data and Private Label Bids

12 October 2026  ·  9 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Most writing about bidding assumes you are selling a service. Scored questions, a method statement, a named team, a day rate. Selling a physical product into a buyer's range is a different exercise. The document that decides it is mostly data: a long attribute template, a product specification, a cost model, a nutrition and allergen declaration, audit evidence for the site that will make the line, and a sample that has to arrive in a particular week. The persuasive paragraph matters. It is the smallest part of the pack.

That changes where the work sits and where it goes wrong. Suppliers in food, drink, household and personal care rarely lose a listing because the story was weak. They lose it because a field disagreed with what is printed on the pack, because a certificate had lapsed, because the cost model rested on an input price that moved while the submission waited, or because the pack went in late while somebody retyped the same product into yet another template.

The review calendar is set a year ahead and does not move

A grocery buyer runs category and range reviews on a fixed clock. The window is set long before anyone asks you to submit, and it closes whether or not your specification is signed off. A supplier with listings across a grocery buyer, a discounter and a foodservice distributor is working to several of these calendars at once, and they overlap.

This is the least interesting part of bidding here and one of the most expensive to get wrong, which makes it good work for an agent. Hold the calendar as structured data: which customer, which category, which of your lines are up for review, which submission date. Then count backwards from each date through the sample deadline, the artwork deadline, the cost sign off and the data cut off, and say what has to start this week. The useful output is not a reminder. It is a list of the lines whose evidence will not be ready in time, produced early enough to do something about it.

Hundreds of attribute fields, and a file that rejects on one value

Buyer templates arrive as spreadsheets with hundreds of columns. Case and pallet dimensions, layers per pallet, barcodes at each packaging level, shelf life on delivery, storage temperature, country of origin and country of last processing, packaging material and weight by component, recycling labelling, net content declarations. Much of it exists somewhere inside your business already, in a specification system, a master data record and a few spreadsheets that one person maintains.

Mapping your record onto somebody else's column names is repetitive, high volume and checkable, which is the shape of task an agent handles well. Give it the template, let it map fields it recognises, fill what it can source, validate against the template's own rules for format and unit, and produce a short list of what it could not find.

The discipline that makes this safe is simple. An unsourced field is marked unknown and left for a person. It is never filled with a plausible value. A guessed case weight looks identical to a correct one in a spreadsheet, and the first time anyone notices is at the depot.

Three versions of one declaration, and only one is on the pack

Allergen, nutrition, origin and packaging data exists in at least three places: the current product specification, the artwork actually printed on the pack, and whatever you submitted to this buyer last cycle. They are supposed to agree. Often they do not, and usually for a legitimate reason nobody wrote down, such as a reformulation, a new supplier for one ingredient, or a packaging change made for weight that altered a recycling claim.

The submission is where that divergence surfaces, and late. Comparing three versions of the same declaration and reporting every difference is exactly what a model is good at, and it is work people do badly because it is dull and the documents are long.

What follows the comparison is not automatable. Deciding which version is correct, whether the specification or the artwork is now wrong, and whether a claim still has evidence behind it is a technical and regulatory judgement with a named owner. The agent's job is to put the three side by side and refuse to let the difference pass quietly.

Bidding to make a product that carries somebody else's name

A private label tender inverts the usual pitch. You are not selling your brand. You are tendering to manufacture against a brief that already specifies the product: a benchmark to match, a target specification, tolerances, a packaging format, sometimes a fixed pack size you do not currently run.

So the questions change. They are about capability and control rather than consumer appeal. Line capacity and current utilisation. Changeover time and minimum run length. Where the second source is if a line goes down. Whether you can hold specification at a volume well above anything you make today. How you handle a recipe that is the buyer's property.

An agent can assemble most of these answers from your own site and production records, and hold the brief as a checklist against what each plant can actually do now rather than what it could do after investment. Committing to a run length, a capacity or an exclusivity term is a commercial decision, and it should look like one on the page.

The cost model moves while the submission sits with the buyer

A bid cost is built on inputs that will not sit still: raw material, packaging, energy, freight, labour. You submit a price, and you may be asked to hold it for a period that starts after the inputs have already changed.

The structure is where an agent helps. Every input named rather than buried in a formula, with its source and the date it was quoted. Quoted inputs distinguished from estimated ones. Assumptions written in plain language where the person signing can read them. Then, when an input moves, the model re-runs and reports which lines now fall outside the assumption they were priced on, so the question reaches commercial while the bid is still open.

The price itself, the hold period, and the mechanism for raising cost later are commitments a person makes and lives with. An agent that prices a bid is a liability. An agent that shows you the bid you are about to submit no longer matches its own inputs is useful every cycle.

Site audits, certifications and the question of which factory

Technical and ethical audit evidence attaches to a site, not to a company. A submission names the site that will make the line, so the audit report, the certification, the open corrective actions and the expiry dates all have to belong to that site. Suppliers with several plants, and anyone using a co-manufacturer, get this wrong under deadline, and the error voids a submission that would otherwise have been competitive.

Index the evidence by site and by standard, with issue date, expiry date and issuing body. Check the buyer's stated requirement against what you hold. Then the rule that does the work: a requirement is not marked satisfied when the document behind it has lapsed, is in a renewal cycle, or belongs to a different site. Where there is a genuine gap, the output is the gap and the lead time to close it, which is a far more useful answer than a filled box.

A new line submission is a forecast with a plan attached

New product submissions ask what the line will sell, what it will take sales from, who buys it, where it belongs on shelf, and what you will spend supporting it. The promotional plan attached to that commits you to activity across the year ahead.

Internal consistency is the common failure and the obvious place for a machine. Does the volume in the promotional plan match the volume the cost model was built on. Does the launch date match the artwork and sample dates. Is the support described in the narrative the support that was actually priced. Is the pack format in the submission the format in the specification. These are checks a person can do and usually runs out of time to do.

Samples and artwork run on a different clock

Samples and artwork have physical lead times that do not compress for anybody. Artwork needs a final declaration, which needs a final specification, which needs the ingredient decisions closed. An agent that holds that dependency order and counts backwards from the submission date earns more here than one that drafts copy.

The same buyer, slightly different questions, every cycle

The pack is never quite the pack you answered last time. Fields are renamed, columns reordered, a new section appears on packaging recyclability or carbon reporting, a question about labour standards is asked in a different form. Almost all of it is the same material, and you cannot safely assume any of it is.

This decides how to build the thing. An agent that answers from your live source records, with each answer traceable to the record it came from, survives a changed template. An agent that pattern matches against last year's submission will cheerfully ship last year's data into this year's review, formatted perfectly.

So maintenance is the permanent job, not the build. After each submission, ingest the final version, compare it against the source records, and propose the updates. Age every answer and attachment, route each to the technical or commercial owner who can confirm it, and mark unreviewed content as unreviewed rather than serving it as approved.

Where AI fails here

A filled field reads the same whether it was sourced or invented. This is the central risk, and the only defence is a hard rule that unknown stays unknown.

Labelling and regulatory judgement stays with the technical owner. Whether a claim is permitted, whether a declaration is correct, and whether a change needs re-testing are not retrieval questions.

The buyer relationship is not in the document set. Why this line, in this category, instead of what is already listed, comes from conversations nobody wrote down.

Price, capacity and exclusivity are human commitments. So is the decision to accept a specification you cannot comfortably hold.

Physical deadlines do not yield to software. A sample still has to be made, packed and sent.

Thin source data gives retrieval nothing to work with. If your product record exists as a different spreadsheet per customer, fixing that is the first project, not a by-product of another one.

Pick one line review and rehearse it

Take one product, one customer, and one review window that has already closed, so nothing is at stake. Rebuild the submission with the agent: map the buyer template to your records, pull the declaration from the current specification, compare it against the artwork and against what you sent last cycle, assemble the site evidence with expiry dates, and re-run the cost model from named and dated inputs.

Then put it next to what you actually submitted. The differences are the measurement. If there are none, you have a clean record and a cheaper process from here. If there are several, you have just found what this year's review will find, with time left to fix it.
