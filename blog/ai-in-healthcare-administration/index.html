AI in Healthcare Administration: Giving Clinicians Their Hours Back

28 August 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Ask any clinician where their day goes and the answer is rarely "patients". It goes into visit notes finished at nine in the evening. It goes into prior authorisation forms, coding queries from the billing team, referral letters, and the slow work of chasing a missing lab result or an unsigned document. Your administrative teams carry the same weight from the other side: approvals to prepare, claims to scrub, inboxes to triage, and follow ups that die quietly because nobody had time to send the third reminder.

The frustrating part is that most of this work follows rules. It reads information from one system, applies known criteria, and writes a result into another system. That is exactly the shape of work AI agents do well. The complication in healthcare is that none of the underlying data may leave the organisation, which rules out most off the shelf tools. The answer is not to avoid AI. It is to deploy it privately, inside your own boundary, wired into the systems you already run. That is the work we do at Adopt AI Partners, and this article walks through what it looks like in practice.

Drafting clinical documentation, not just transcribing it

Ambient scribing gets the attention, but the useful version goes further than turning speech into text. A documentation agent reads the encounter transcript or the clinician's rough dictation, plus the relevant context it is permitted to see: the problem list, active medications, recent results, and the note template your organisation uses for that visit type.

It then drafts the note in your format, in your house style, with the structured fields filled in rather than buried in prose. It decides what belongs in the assessment versus the plan, flags anything it heard that contradicts the record, and writes the draft back into the EHR as a pending note.

The handoff point is fixed and non negotiable: the clinician reviews and signs. The agent never files a note on its own. What changes is that the clinician starts from a ninety percent draft instead of a blank screen, and the review takes minutes instead of the evening.

Preparing prior authorisations before anyone asks

Prior authorisation is chasing work disguised as clinical work. An agent handles the disguise. When an order is placed that your rules say needs authorisation, the agent reads the order, the payer's published criteria for that service, and the patient's chart for the evidence those criteria require: diagnoses, prior treatments tried, imaging, relevant notes.

It assembles the request, citing where in the chart each piece of evidence came from, and writes the draft into your authorisation queue. Where the evidence is complete and the criteria are clearly met, the packet is ready to submit. Where something is missing, the agent says exactly what is missing and who can supply it, instead of the request bouncing back weeks later.

A person submits the request and owns any peer to peer conversation with the payer. The agent's job is to make sure that person never starts from an empty form, and to keep watching the queue so that a pending authorisation gets followed up on schedule rather than when someone remembers.

Coding review before the claim leaves the building

Coding queries are a tax on clinician time: a message asking for clarification, days after the encounter, about something the clinician no longer remembers clearly. An agent reduces the tax at the source. After a note is signed, it reads the documentation and the codes attached to the encounter, checks them against your coding rules and payer specific edits, and looks for the usual failure modes: a code the documentation does not support, documentation that supports a code nobody entered, missing specificity, mismatched laterality.

Clean encounters pass through untouched. Where the agent finds a discrepancy, it writes a specific query, quoting the exact sentence in the note that raised the question, and routes it to the coder or the clinician depending on your workflow. Coders stop spending their day on encounters that were already fine and spend it on the ones that genuinely need judgement. The agent proposes; a certified coder decides. That boundary matters for compliance and it does not move.

Referral intake and triage

Referrals arrive as faxes, portal messages, and PDFs of wildly varying quality, and someone has to read every one. An intake agent does the first read. It extracts the patient details, the referring clinician, the reason for referral, and the attached results, then checks the package against your acceptance criteria for that service: is the required imaging attached, is the referral in scope, is the insurance information present.

Complete referrals get registered, categorised by urgency according to your triage rules, and placed in the right queue with a structured summary on top, so the clinician doing triage reads one paragraph instead of a fourteen page fax. Incomplete referrals generate a draft request back to the referrer listing precisely what is missing. Anything ambiguous, and anything the rules classify as potentially urgent, goes straight to a person. The agent never declines a referral and never assigns clinical urgency on its own where your protocol says a human must.

Chasing, so your staff do not have to

A large share of administrative time is not doing tasks but keeping them alive: the unsigned order, the lab result that never came back, the records request a hospital has ignored for three weeks, the patient who has not booked the follow up. This work is ideal for an agent because it is mostly memory and persistence.

The agent watches for items that have passed their expected date, checks the systems to confirm the item is genuinely still outstanding, and then acts within limits you set: a reminder to the right internal queue, a draft chaser letter for a records request, a task for the scheduling team. Every nudge is logged, every draft that leaves the organisation is reviewed by a person first, and anything still stuck after a defined number of attempts escalates to a named owner rather than looping forever.

Inbox and message triage

Clinician and departmental inboxes mix the trivial with the urgent, and the cost of misfiling is high in both directions. A triage agent reads incoming messages, results, and portal queries, classifies them against your routing rules, and attaches the context the recipient will need: for a result, the relevant order and the trend; for a patient query, the last visit summary.

Routine items are routed with a suggested draft reply attached for a person to approve. Anything that pattern matches a clinical red flag under your protocol is escalated immediately and conservatively; the agent is tuned to over escalate rather than the reverse. The result is not an empty inbox. It is an inbox where the top of the pile is genuinely the most important thing in it.

Private deployment: nothing leaves the organisation

Everything above depends on one architectural decision. Patient data cannot flow into a third party's multi tenant product, so the agents run inside your boundary: your cloud tenancy or your data centre, inside your network controls, under your identity and access management.

The practical rules we build to are simple to state. The agent authenticates to your systems as a service account with the minimum access the workflow requires, and no more. Data is processed within your environment and is not used to train anyone's models. Every read and every write is logged to your audit infrastructure, so you can answer "what did the agent see and what did it do" for any record, on any day. Retention follows your policy, not a vendor's default. And the integration surface is your existing one: the EHR's APIs and interface standards you already support, not a parallel copy of your data warehouse.

This is also what makes governance sign off achievable. Your privacy and security teams are not evaluating a black box service; they are reviewing a system that lives under controls they already own.

Where to start

Do not start with the hardest clinical workflow. Start with one process that is high volume, rule bound, and painful, where a wrong answer is caught by the existing human review step rather than reaching a patient. Prior authorisation preparation and referral intake are common first choices for exactly that reason.

Then run it as a real deployment, not a demo. Define the handoff points with the people who own the process today. Measure the baseline honestly: minutes per item, backlog age, rework rate. Run the agent in draft only mode until its output survives contact with your reviewers, then widen its authority one decision at a time. In our experience the teams that succeed treat the first agent as an operational change with software attached, not the other way round.

Where AI fails here

It is worth being direct about the failure modes, because they define where the human handoffs go.

Agents fail when the source data is wrong; they will faithfully draft an authorisation from an outdated problem list. They fail on genuine edge cases, the referral that fits no category and the payer rule that contradicts itself, which is why every workflow needs an explicit route to a person rather than a forced guess. Language models can state things confidently that are not so, which is why nothing here files, submits, or replies without review wherever the output carries clinical or financial consequence. And no agent fixes a broken process; automating a workflow that produces the wrong outcome just produces it faster. Deployments that ignore these limits erode trust in a week. Deployments that design for them earn wider authority month by month.

The point of all this is not headcount. It is that your clinicians trained for a decade to do work that only they can do, and much of their week is currently spent on work that software can now carry, safely, inside your walls, with people making every call that matters. The organisations that get those hours back will feel it in retention, in access, and in the quality of the notes themselves.
