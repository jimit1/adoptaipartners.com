AI Use Cases in Construction: Submittals, Variations and the Document War

Construction runs on documents in a way few other industries do. Drawings, submittals, RFIs, variations, payment applications, test certificates, warranties, notices. Every one of them is contractual, and every one of them is time critical. Miss a submittal response window and the programme slips. Miss a notice deadline on a variation and you may have surrendered your entitlement to be paid for work you have already done. Lose track of which drawing revision is current and someone pours concrete against a superseded detail.

Most of the people managing this flood are engineers and quantity surveyors who did not train to be document controllers. They spend their evenings logging transmittals, chasing overdue responses and reconciling registers, and the project still bleeds information through the gaps. This is the document war, and it is exactly the kind of high volume, rules driven, deadline bound work that production AI agents are now good at. Not a chatbot bolted onto your intranet. An agent that reads the documents, applies your procedures, writes back into your systems and hands the judgement calls to a person.

Here are the use cases we see working, with enough detail that you can judge whether they fit your projects.

Submittal intake, logging and first pass review

A submittal arrives as an email attachment, a common data environment upload or a portal notification. The agent reads the package: the transmittal sheet, the product data, the shop drawings, the covering letter. It extracts the specification section, the revision, the supplier, and what the submittal actually claims to demonstrate. It then checks that against the submittal register and the specification clause it responds to.

What it decides: whether the package is administratively complete. Is the right spec section referenced, are all the sheets listed on the transmittal actually attached, is this a resubmittal that should reference prior review comments, does the stated product match what the specification calls for at the level of model numbers and standards cited. What it writes back: a register entry, a due date calculated from the contract, and a structured summary for the reviewing engineer that lists what changed since the last revision.

What it hands to a person: the technical judgement. The agent never approves a submittal. It gets a clean, pre-checked package in front of the right reviewer on day one instead of day six, with the boring completeness checks already done, so the reviewer opens a summary instead of a 200 page PDF.

Variation and change event capture

Variations rarely announce themselves. They surface in site diaries, meeting minutes, RFI answers that quietly change scope, and instructions given verbally then confirmed in a one line email. By the time the commercial team formalises them, the notice window in the contract may already be closing.

An agent watching your correspondence and meeting records can flag candidate change events as they occur. It reads incoming instructions and RFI responses, compares them against the current scope baseline and the drawing set, and asks a simple question: does this direct work that differs from what was priced. When the answer looks like yes, it drafts the change event record, attaches the source correspondence, identifies the relevant contract clause and the notice deadline, and puts a draft notification in front of your commercial manager.

The person decides whether it is genuinely a variation, what it is worth and whether to submit the notice. The agent's job is to make sure nothing that smells like a change dies unlogged in an inbox. On a project with thousands of emails a week, that watchfulness is not something you can staff your way to.

Drawing revision control and clash of record

Every contractor has a story about work built to a superseded drawing. The failure mode is rarely the CDE itself. It is the gap between the CDE and the way information actually moves: a subcontractor working from a drawing saved to a desktop three weeks ago, a detail referenced in an RFI answer that has since been revised.

An agent can hold the current-revision picture and defend it. When a new revision lands, it reads the revision cloud and the change description, identifies which open RFIs, submittals and work packages reference the superseded sheet, and notifies the owners of each. When outgoing correspondence cites a drawing number, the agent checks the revision cited against the register and flags a mismatch before the document goes out. It writes back to the register and to the affected records, and it escalates to the document controller when it finds a reference it cannot resolve.

This is unglamorous work. It is also the difference between a register that is technically correct and a project team that is actually working from current information.

RFI triage and draft responses

Not every RFI needs a design team. A meaningful share are answered by information that already exists: a detail on another sheet, a clause in the specification, a prior RFI on the same question. An agent that has read the full drawing set, the specification and the RFI history can triage incoming questions in minutes.

For questions answerable from existing information, it drafts a response citing the exact sheet, detail or clause, and routes the draft to the responsible engineer for review and sign off. For questions that genuinely need design input, it routes them to the right consultant with the relevant background attached, so the consultant is not spending their fee budget rediscovering context. It tracks the response clock either way and chases before the deadline, not after.

Nothing leaves the building without a human signature. The gain is that your engineers review drafts instead of composing from a blank page, and your design team only sees the questions that need them.

Payment applications and certificate reconciliation

Assessing a payment application means reconciling the claimed quantities and percentages against measured progress, prior certificates, agreed variations and contra charges. It is deadline bound, because most contracts and payment legislation give you a fixed window to certify or issue a payment notice, and silence has consequences.

An agent can do the assembly. It reads the application, lines it up item by item against the previous certificate and the variation account, flags items claimed above the agreed value or ahead of recorded progress, and checks that the supporting documents the contract requires are actually attached. It drafts the certificate or payment notice with every deviation from the claim itemised and referenced, and it puts the draft in front of the quantity surveyor with days still on the clock.

The commercial judgement about what to certify remains human. What disappears is the late night spreadsheet reconciliation that currently precedes it.

Test certificates, warranties and the handover file

Handover is where years of document discipline get audited at once. Operations and maintenance manuals, test and commissioning certificates, warranties, as-built drawings. Chasing these from dozens of subcontractors at the end of the job is miserable, and gaps discovered at practical completion delay the certificate and the final account.

Run it as a continuous process instead. From the start of the project the agent maintains the required-documents matrix: which certificates, from which subcontractor, for which system, in which format. As documents arrive it reads them, checks the obvious fields, that the certificate matches the equipment schedule, that dates and signatures are present, that the standard cited is the one specified, and files them against the matrix. It chases the gaps monthly, by named document, and gives you a live completion percentage per subcontractor. At handover you are printing a file, not conducting an archaeology dig.

Notice and obligation tracking across the contract

Behind all of these sits the contract itself: dozens of time bound obligations, notice periods, condition precedents and response windows scattered across the conditions. Few project teams can recite them, and the cost of missing one can dwarf the cost of the underlying event.

An agent can read the executed contract once, extract every obligation with a clock attached, and then watch the project's correspondence and records for the triggers. Ground conditions claim mentioned in a site diary, weather event logged, instruction received: the agent connects the event to the clause, states the deadline, and drafts the notice for a person to review and issue. It is a contracts-aware early warning system that never gets busy, and it works for both sides of the contract, tracking the obligations owed to you as carefully as the ones you owe.

Where to start

Do not start with the whole document war. Pick one flow that is high volume, painful and easy to measure. For most organisations that is submittal intake or RFI triage, because the inputs are standardised, the procedure is written down, and the before and after is visible within weeks. Run the agent in shadow mode first: it processes everything, a person checks everything, and you measure agreement. Widen its authority only as the evidence supports it. Keep the human sign off on anything contractual permanently. The goal is not to remove people from the loop. It is to remove the document handling from the people.

Where AI fails here

Honesty matters more than enthusiasm in this domain, so here is where these systems genuinely struggle. Scanned, skewed or handwritten documents still produce extraction errors, and old drawings are worse than new ones. Ambiguous contract drafting defeats automated clause interpretation, because it defeats human interpretation too, and an agent should say so rather than guess. Agents cannot assess build quality, negotiate a final account or make the judgement call on a borderline variation, and any vendor telling you otherwise is selling. They are also only as good as the systems they connect to: if your registers are already wrong, an agent will faithfully keep them wrong until someone corrects the baseline. And they need real deployment engineering, permissions, audit trails and rollback, because a wrong automated notice is a contractual act. Treat these systems as tireless, literal minded document staff who escalate when unsure, and design the escalation paths before you design anything else.

The pattern across every use case above is the same: the agent reads what your team currently reads, applies the procedure your team already follows, writes into the systems you already run, and stops at the point where judgement starts. That pattern is buildable today inside your environment, on your data, under your sign off rules.
