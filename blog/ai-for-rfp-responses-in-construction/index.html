AI for RFP Responses in Construction: Prequalification, Returnables and the Tender Deadline

27 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

The portal closes at noon on Friday. It is Thursday evening, the estimator is still resolving a provisional sum, the planner is reissuing the programme narrative because the sequence changed on Tuesday, and someone is working down the returnables schedule with a highlighter because two required certificates are in a folder nobody can name and one expired last month. The quality submission was good on Monday. Since then the client issued an addendum that changed a phasing assumption three answers were built on, and nobody has checked which ones.

That is the shape of the work. Not writing, exactly. Assembling a contractual document out of other people's material, under a deadline that does not move, against a marking scheme somebody else wrote.

Why a construction bid is not a software proposal

A software proposal is persuasion. A tender submission is closer to an offer capable of acceptance. What you write into the method statement can end up as a contract document, and what you price is what you are held to. The two halves are marked separately, and the quality score can be worth as much as the money.

Then the deadline, which does not care that the file was too large to upload. Late is not late. Late is not a bid. Speed that costs accuracy in the pricing document is not a saving.

Prequalification and framework applications

Most bid teams answer the same corporate questions several times a year. Turnover and accounts. Insurance levels and expiry dates. Accident frequency and the last enforcement action. Environmental management arrangements. The questions are broadly stable across the prequalification schemes your clients use, and each asks for them in a slightly different shape, with a different word limit and its own evidence to attach.

This is the cleanest use of an agent in the process, because the mapping is mechanical: match each question to the maintained corporate answer, adapt it to the length asked for, and produce the attachment list, flagging anything whose date has passed. What goes wrong is rarely the writing. It is an answer out of date, attached in the wrong version, or shortened by hand under pressure until it no longer says the thing that earned the mark.

Turning the document set into two lists

A tender pack arrives as a pile: employer's requirements, drawings, schedules, the pricing document, the preliminaries, contract particulars, the amendments to the standard form, the instructions to tenderers. Somebody has to turn that into two lists. What has to be returned, and what has to be priced.

An agent reading the whole set can draft both, with a reference back to the clause or schedule cell each item came from. That reference is the point. A list where every line points at where it was asked for can be checked in an hour rather than a day.

The scope behind each pricing line lives elsewhere, in the employer's requirements and on the drawings. Pulling those specification references together is slow work that estimators currently do at midnight.

The returnables schedule as a compliance problem

A missing certificate fails a winning bid. So does a form signed by the wrong person, a file over the size limit, a document in the wrong format, or a schedule returned unsigned. This is a checklist problem where the cost of one omission is the whole submission.

Treat the returnables list as a live object rather than a document. Every item has a required format, an owner, a state and a source, and an agent can re-check that against the current issue of the instructions to tenderers, which get amended. What it cannot do is upload the files or sign them.

An answer library that knows where each claim came from

Reuse is not the problem. Unexamined reuse is. Every library holds claims that were true on the project they were written for and are not true now: a site manager who has left, a logistics arrangement that suited a city centre site and makes no sense on a rural one.

The useful structure attaches each passage to its origin. Which project, which client, which score it received if you know. Then a first draft comes from material you have already stood behind, with project specific claims marked for replacement rather than quietly carried across.

Choosing references against the stated tests

The criteria usually say what relevance means: value band, sector, procurement route, contract form, occupied site, constrained logistics. Reference selection still defaults to the most impressive job, or the one the bid manager remembers. Ranking your completed projects against the stated tests changes which three you put forward, and an agent can give the reason for each.

Method statements and programme narrative

The method statement should be an account of how you intend to build the thing, written from the planner's sequence and the estimator's resource assumptions. The words come last. When they come first you get a narrative that contradicts the programme, and evaluators who read both notice.

An agent working from the planner's own material, the sequence, the durations, the logistics plan, can produce a narrative that matches it, in the register that scored well for this client before. Clients differ in what they reward, so a record of which submissions scored well with which evaluators beats general advice about writing.

Quality, social value and sustainability submissions

The criteria are published. The weightings usually are too. And answers are still written to the wrong question, because the team writes about what it is proud of rather than what is being marked. If the criterion asks how you will manage interfaces with the operational parts of the site, an answer about your national supply chain scores nothing.

So the mechanical check earns its place: for each criterion, which paragraphs address it, and which published sub points are not addressed anywhere. Social value submissions in particular promise more than delivery can support, so draft them against what your projects have actually delivered.

Safety and environmental answers grounded in the site records

These are the most reused and the least checked. The strong version is not a policy restatement. It is specific: what the last similar job did, what went wrong, what changed afterwards. That material sits in incident reports, inspection findings and toolbox records, and it is not normally in front of whoever writes the answer. An agent with access to those records can draft from them and refuse to assert what they do not support.

Qualifications, assumptions and clarifications

Every bid carries things the team wants out: scope it will not accept, information it did not get, assumptions it had to make to price at all. These live in margin notes and in the estimator's head, and a real proportion never reach the submitted qualifications schedule. Then they are not qualifications. They are risk you priced against and did not exclude.

The discipline is to write them where they survive: in the schedule the client will read, in language that means the same thing in a contract as it did on the call. An agent can collect candidates from the bid team's working notes and present them for decision. Which ones to submit is commercial judgement, because every qualification costs a little compliance.

Contract amendments as a pricing question

The amendments to the standard form matter at bid stage for one reason: they change the number. A risk moved onto you, a cap removed, a notice period shortened, an indemnity widened. Reading them here is not a legal opinion exercise. It is finding what needs pricing, qualifying or walking away from, while there is still time to respond.

An agent comparing the amended document against the unamended standard form produces the list of departures and what each one shifts. The commercial response stays with people. What the tool removes is the chance of a departure nobody read.

Addenda and one version of the truth

Clients reissue documents, sometimes days before return. A revised pricing document, a new drawing, an answer in the clarification log that changes an assumption. The risk is not the change. It is a submission built half on the old version.

Keeping one current view of the pack, with a record of which drafted answers relied on superseded text, is hard by hand and straightforward for a tool holding both versions. The output that matters is short: these four answers and two pricing lines rest on something that has changed.

The settlement and review gate

Before it goes, the bid gets read twice. Once against the criteria, answer by answer, scoring it as the evaluator would. Once against the returnables list, item by item. An agent can do a useful first pass at both and will find omissions a tired reader misses at eleven at night. It cannot do the settlement meeting. The price, the risk allowances, the qualifications and the decision to bid stay with the bid team and the board.

Where AI fails here

It misreads drawings. Language models are unreliable on drawn information, so anything depending on a detail or a dimension needs a person.

It will fill a gap rather than leave one. Asked about a system you have not built, it produces plausible prose, and that is the most dangerous behaviour here, because plausible prose in a submitted method statement can become a contractual obligation. Every generated passage needs an owner who has confirmed it is true of this job.

It does not know the client's unwritten preferences, the site visit, or the conversation the bid director had at a framework event. It has no view on whether the job is worth winning.

It cannot price. Rates, allowances and the final number are not a text generation problem, and treating them as one is how firms lose money slowly.

A single tender you have already lost

Do not pilot this on a live bid. Take one you submitted and lost, and run the agent over the original pack as it arrived. Let it build the returnables list, map the criteria to your submitted answers, flag the departures in the amendments, and mark the claims it thinks were carried over from elsewhere.

Then compare. Did it find the sub criterion you missed? Did it catch the certificate that was out of date? Did it flag the paragraph the evaluator marked down? Did it invent anything, and would the reviewer have caught it?
