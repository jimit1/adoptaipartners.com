AI for Contract Review in Healthcare

17 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Ask a provider organisation what its largest payer contract pays for a common outpatient procedure and you will usually get three answers. Legal has the executed agreement and a folder of amendment letters. Revenue cycle has a rate loaded into the billing system by somebody who has since moved teams. Finance has a budget assumption. Nobody can say which is correct, and the difference between them is money leaving the building every week.

This is what makes contract review in healthcare a different job from contract review anywhere else. A payer agreement is not primarily a risk document to be reviewed once and filed. It is an operating instruction that has to stay accurate in a billing system for years, across amendments nobody negotiated, while a second stack of agreements governs what you pay for supplies, a third governs how you may pay the clinicians who deliver the care, and a fourth governs what happens to the patient data underneath all of it.

Three teams, four filing cabinets, no shared register

Legal owns signature. Revenue cycle owns the consequences of the payer terms but rarely reads them. Supply chain owns purchasing agreements and usually holds them in a separate system entirely. Compliance owns the business associate agreements and the regulatory constraints on physician arrangements. Each function is competent. None holds the whole picture, and the contract that matters to a denial argument on Tuesday sits in a folder the person arguing has never opened.

The first useful thing an agent does here is not analysis. It is building a single structured register from the executed documents wherever they live, with every extracted value citing the clause and document version it came from. Everything below depends on that register existing.

The payer contract is a pricing document nobody has priced

A payer agreement sets out how a claim is meant to be valued, and it does so across several places at once: the base reimbursement methodology, the fee schedule attached as an exhibit, the carve outs for particular service lines or implantable devices, outlier and stop loss provisions for high cost stays, differentials by site of service, and the lesser of language that quietly caps payment at your billed charge.

An agent can turn that structure into machine readable terms: the methodology per service category, the schedule version and its effective dates, each carve out with its trigger, the reduction rules for multiple procedures, and the filing and appeal windows that govern what you can do when payment is wrong. Each field cites its clause. Where the contract is genuinely ambiguous, and payer paper often is, the agent reports the ambiguity rather than resolving it, because a confident wrong rate propagates into thousands of claims.

Extracted terms are testable. Revenue cycle can price a claim as the contract says it should have been priced and compare that against what arrived, which most organisations cannot begin because nobody has written the terms down in a form a system can use.

The documents the contract points at

The clause that causes the most damage is usually the one incorporating the payer's provider manual, medical policies and payment rules by reference, sometimes with a right to amend them on notice. Those documents carry real obligations: coding edits, bundling definitions, referral requirements, submission formats.

An agent that reads only the signed agreement produces a summary that is accurate about what it read and wrong about what you owe. Incorporated documents have to be chased, versioned and read alongside the contract, and anything not located is reported as not located rather than assumed absent.

Amendments that take effect because nobody replied

Payer amendments frequently arrive as letters rather than negotiations. A revised fee schedule, a new policy, a changed reimbursement methodology for one service line, effective in ninety days, deemed accepted unless you object in writing within a stated window.

These land in shared inboxes and get read after the payments change. An agent reading payer correspondence on arrival can answer one question: does this alter a term already in the register. If it does, it produces a short change note naming the affected terms, the objection deadline, the effective date and the owner who has to decide. It proposes the register update and never applies it silently, and it never sends the objection. A person does that.

A rate that changes in the contract also has to change in the billing system, and in most organisations that handoff is unmanaged. Extraction that stops at a document does nothing.

Group purchasing and supplier agreements

Supply chain contracting fails in the other direction: the terms are usually favourable and the organisation does not collect them.

Group purchasing agreements price by tier, and the tier depends on committed volume or compliance with a specified product portfolio. Pricing is activated per facility through letters of participation. Crossover happens when the same item sits on a local agreement and a national one at different prices. Rebates accrue against conditions somebody has to evidence. Price protection clauses cap increases, and suppliers raise prices anyway.

An agent reads the agreement set and extracts the entitlement: item, contracted price, tier conditions, effective and expiry dates, price protection terms, rebate triggers, and the facilities the pricing covers. Compared against the item master and purchase history, the mismatches surface: items bought off contract that sit on one, prices invoiced above the contracted rate, tiers you qualify for and are not receiving, rebate conditions met and never claimed. That comparison is arithmetic and belongs in ordinary code, with the model doing the reading. The conversation with the supplier stays with a person.

Physician, locum and coverage agreements

Clinician agreements carry constraints ordinary commercial contracts do not. Compensation arrangements are bounded by regulatory requirements around fair market value and commercial reasonableness, and the structure of a productivity or medical directorship payment matters as much as the number in it. That is a compliance judgement with legal sign off, not a task for an agent.

What an agent can carry is the administration around it, which is substantial and mostly ignored. It extracts the terms that drive operations: the compensation model and its measurement basis, productivity thresholds and how they are calculated, call coverage obligations and rates, notice periods, restrictive covenants with their geography and duration, termination triggers, malpractice cover and whether tail insurance is required and who pays for it, and the credentialing conditions attached to the arrangement.

For locum and agency cover the same extraction answers questions otherwise asked in a hurry: the agreed rate against what the agency invoices, whether direct hire is permitted and at what fee, what indemnity the agency owes. Reconciling timesheets against contracted rates gets skipped when a department is short staffed, which is when the invoices are largest.

Business associate agreements and the terms underneath them

Every vendor touching patient data has an agreement governing that access, and in most organisations the list of those vendors and the list of those agreements do not match.

An agent maintains both sides. It extracts the substance rather than confirming a signature exists: permitted uses, subcontractor flow down obligations, the breach notification window and to whom, the security requirements the vendor accepted, where data may be held, the audit rights you hold, and what happens to data on termination. It compares those terms against the vendor inventory and the data each vendor actually receives, then flags the gaps: a live integration with no agreement, a notification window longer than the one you owe onwards, deletion obligations with no evidence they were performed, a subprocessor added without consent. A signed agreement filed correctly proves very little about what a vendor is doing.

Renewals, evergreen terms and the window nobody diarised

Healthcare agreements are unusually prone to renewing themselves. Payer contracts often run evergreen with rates rolling forward, so a below market rate persists because no one triggered the renegotiation window. Supplier agreements auto renew at list. Termination without cause is commonly available on notice to either side, which means your leverage has a date on it.

An agent working from the register raises each of these with the named owner while there is time to act: the renegotiation window opening, the notice period before an auto renewal, the expiry of a price protection term, an insurance certificate lapsing. It states what the clause requires and what happens if the window passes. It never serves a notice, never terminates and never renews.

Where AI fails here

Regulatory judgement is out of scope. Whether a physician compensation arrangement satisfies the applicable rules, whether a referral relationship is permissible, whether a data flow is lawful: these are questions for compliance counsel, and the correct output is an escalation with the wording quoted.

Interpretation is not extraction. Whether a payer's bundling definition covers a particular service, whether a reduction the contract does not mention is nonetheless permitted by an incorporated policy, whether a clause means what the payer says it means in a dispute: none of these is a reading task.

The register decays if it is not maintained. A term extracted from a superseded exhibit is worse than none at all, because people will act on it. Amendments, schedule versions, participation letters and policy updates have to flow in continuously, and the system should show the version every value came from.

Bad source documents defeat it. Scanned schedules, faxed amendment letters and fee exhibits running to thousands of lines extract less reliably than clean digital documents, and accuracy on those inputs should be measured separately rather than averaged into a headline number.

And no agent fixes the organisational problem. If legal, revenue cycle and supply chain do not agree who owns a decision, a shared register makes the disagreement visible rather than resolving it. That is progress, but it is not the same thing.

Where to start

Begin with extraction on one payer contract that matters, including its exhibits and every amendment, and have someone who knows the account check the output line by line. You will learn how reliable extraction is on your own documents, and how far the contract has drifted from the rates loaded in your billing system.

Then widen by document type rather than by ambition. The supplier set is usually the fastest place to show a number, because the comparison against purchase history is arithmetic. The renewal calendar is the cheapest, because it needs only dates and owners.
