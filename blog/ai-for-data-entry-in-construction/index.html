AI for Data Entry in Construction

18 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A wagon arrives at the gate at first light. The gateman signs for the load, tears off his copy of the ticket and drops it in a plastic wallet, where it spends an hour on a wet bench. On Friday the surveyor keys the lot into the cost system, several of them again into the main contractor's portal, and sends a spreadsheet to the commercial manager.

Nobody in that chain did a poor job. One fact, a load of blocks arriving on a Tuesday, was typed by three people into three places, from paper never meant to be read twice. Construction data entry is not a volume problem. It is a duplication problem with a deadline.

Nobody on a site was hired to key records

The people creating these records are gatemen, foremen, site engineers and site managers, judged on whether the works progress. The keying happens at the end of a long day, so fields get whatever clears validation and the part that matters stays in somebody's head. Then the document reaches an administrator who cannot question it without finding the person who wrote it. That handover is where most of it goes wrong. Not carelessness. Distance.

The paper that turns into money before a cut-off

A payment application has a date, and it is not negotiable. A ticket keyed the day after it waits a month. A day work sheet without a signature is not money, it is paper. A notice sent late can forfeit an entitlement that was otherwise sound.

The clock belongs to the contract and to somebody else's calendar, usually the main contractor's. Volume you can absorb by working late. A cut-off you cannot.

Timesheets that arrive as photographs of paper

Subcontractor labour is recorded on a paper sheet and sent in as a photograph through a messaging app, often at an angle, often at dusk. Operative names are spelled the way the foreman heard them, not the way payroll holds them. Hours sit in columns that do not total. Activities are shorthand that made sense to the person holding the pen.

Reading those sheets into structured lines is tractable, and the right output keeps a crop of the original image against each line, so the administrator confirms a few characters against a picture instead of typing the sheet blind.

The operative who appears twice on the same day

The useful part is not the extraction, it is the contradiction. One operative shown on two activities for a full shift each. The same name on two sites on the same date. A week with a day lost to weather the diary already records.

A system cannot resolve any of those. It can put them in front of the right person the day the sheet arrives, while the foreman still remembers, not three weeks later when the cost report looks odd.

A delivery only exists if the paper came back

The delivery ticket is the proof that material arrived. Without it there is a supplier invoice and an argument. It is signed in the open by whoever was nearest, and the top copy leaves with the driver.

Matching that ticket to a purchase order and a goods received record is work a system should do, including a supplier reference on no order and a partial load amended in pen. The output is a record that reconciles, with the signed ticket behind it, so whoever approves payment sees evidence rather than hunting for it.

The excavator that stayed on hire after it left site

Plant hire runs on two dates and a piece of paper: on hire, off hire, and the request that proves when you asked. A machine sits behind the welfare unit for weeks because the sequence changed and nobody told the hire desk. An off hire is agreed verbally and never keyed, so invoices keep arriving and the credit becomes a negotiation rather than a correction.

Reconciling hire records against invoices and against what is on site suits an agent well. The output that matters is the exception: this machine has been on hire since spring, the last activity booked against it finished a month ago, and no off hire request exists.

The diary written at the end of a twelve hour day

A site diary records weather, labour, plant, deliveries, instructions, stoppages and anything that interfered with progress. Two years later it is the contemporaneous record that decides whether a delay claim stands up.

It is also written when the writer wants to go home, which is why so many diaries say nothing but wet, slow progress. A system already holding the timesheets, tickets and plant records can assemble most of a day's entry and ask the site manager only for what no record contains, which is what stopped and why.

A pour has a record, a cube and a certificate

A pour is recorded by location, element, mix, volume and time against a delivery docket. Cubes go to a laboratory and the result returns days later on a separate report. Steel arrives with mill certificates. All of it belongs in the quality file against an element buried under later work long before anyone audits it.

The failure is rarely that the test was not done, it is that the result never got filed against the pour. An agent can match laboratory reports and certificates to pour records and name the gaps while the work is still accessible, the only time a gap is cheap to close.

What was instructed, and what was only mentioned

Day work sheets and variation instructions are the paper trail for extra money, and the most contested records on any project. A verbal instruction on a Tuesday walk round. An email that says can you just. A marked up drawing handed over at a meeting. Someone has to turn that into a record with a date, a reference and a name, before the window closes.

A system can capture all of it and prompt for the confirmation that turns a conversation into an instruction. It must not conclude on its own that the instruction was given.

Labour returns, where a slip becomes a statutory problem

Labour returns feed payroll and the tax deductions you make from subcontractors, where a keying error is not an inconvenience to be fixed next month. The wrong deduction rate, a subcontractor whose verification status was never updated, a payment statement that does not reflect what was paid: these are matters you answer for personally.

Extraction and cross checking help here. Submission is a different act, and belongs to a person with the authority to make it.

Cards, inductions and the date that stops somebody working

Induction records, competency cards and training expiries decide who is allowed on site. They arrive as photographs taken at the turnstile and expire quietly. The check worth having looks forward: this card expires before the works it covers are due to finish, and this operative is inducted for an activity the method statement does not cover.

Measured on site, then measured again for the valuation

A site manager records lengths, counts and areas as the work goes in, because that is the only moment the information exists. The surveyor preparing the monthly valuation will not take those figures as they stand, and measures again from drawings. Both are right, and the work gets done twice.

The contribution here is narrow. Carry the site record forward in structured form with its photographs and date, so the surveyor is checking a quantity against evidence rather than collecting it again. The figure still belongs to the surveyor.

Writing the record into all of them, not one of them

This is what decides whether any of the above is worth doing. Most tools read a document and populate one system, leaving the duplication where it was. An agent should produce one validated record and write it into every place that needs it: the cost system, the client's portal, the quality file and the spreadsheet the commercial team relies on.

Where a portal has no interface worth the name, the agent works the screens a person would, and records what it entered and when. The test is not how many documents were read. It is whether anybody had to type the same fact twice.

Registers retyped because a consultant issued a revision

Drawing and document registers show the problem at its plainest. A revision arrives as a transmittal, somebody retypes the number, revision and status into the site register, and if a step is missed a joiner works to a superseded sheet. The by-product matters more than the register: the revisions nobody on site has acknowledged.

Chasing the missing field while somebody still remembers

A record with a blank field is not finished, and the cost of filling it rises every hour. An agent reading an incomplete timesheet should ask the foreman who sent it, in the channel he already uses, the same day. That removes more rework than any amount of extraction accuracy.

Where AI fails here

Measurement for valuation is a quantity surveyor's judgement, not an extraction. What is included, and what the contract lets you claim this period, is an argument you will lose if a machine produced the number.

An agent cannot decide whether a variation was instructed. It can hold the evidence and show you the pattern. The conclusion stays with the person who will have to defend it.

A signature it cannot read is a missing signature and must be treated as one. The failure to avoid is a system that reads a smudge plausibly and lets a payment proceed.

Cost code allocation can be suggested from history, but it has to be owned by a person. Cost codes carry commercial meaning the document does not show, and a quietly miscoded month distorts every report built on it.

Statutory payroll and tax submissions need a human on the hook. Prepare, reconcile, flag, and stop there.

Wet and faint carbon copies have a real limit. Some paper is unreadable, and the right behaviour is to say so and route it to a person, not to guess.

Where to start

Pick the document type that causes the most rework, usually delivery tickets or subcontractor timesheets, and take it end to end on one project for a month. Read it, validate it, write it into every system that needs it, and measure it against the corrections your team made last quarter, because those are your real errors.

Do not begin with measurement, variations or anything ending in a statutory submission. Begin where the same fact is typed more than once, prove it can be typed once, then move along the chain.
