AI for RFP Responses in Hospitality

14 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

By the middle of October the corporate inbox has stopped being a queue and become a backlog. A travel management company wants negotiated rates for next year at three of your hotels, on a spreadsheet of its own design. A request platform has pushed through a batch of bids overnight, each needing a rate, a last room availability answer, a cancellation policy from a controlled list and an amenity grid filled in.

In the next folder sits a different job. A conference organiser wants a room block over three nights, a plenary room, breakouts, a drinks reception and dinner, with a food and beverage minimum, an attrition clause and a cut off date, for dates provisionally held for somebody else.

Both are called an RFP. One is a high volume pricing exercise against a compressed calendar. The other is a negotiation over space, pattern and terms, running against a function diary that changes hourly. Most of the work in both is retrieving facts about the building, shaping them into somebody else's required format, pricing what you are about to give away, and getting a named human to agree to it.

The season is short and the rate you file in it lasts a year

Transient season compresses the negotiation of your whole corporate base into a few weeks. Every account you hold comes back for review, every account you do not hold is being bid for by the hotel across the square, and the deadlines cluster because the buyers work to one travel year. Being late costs an account off your rate programme until the next cycle.

So an agent earns its place before anyone writes a rate, watching the platforms, the shared sales inbox, consortia notices and your own renewal dates, then holding one forward view of what closes when and which properties are in scope.

Loading the bid means answering in codes, not sentences

Request platforms do not want prose. They want a rate against a rate category, a currency, a date band, a cancellation policy from a controlled list, a tax breakout in the platform's own structure and amenity flags with values. One wants a single rate per room type for the year, another seasonal tiers, another a dynamic discount off a named public rate, which is a different commercial commitment wearing the same label.

Shaping work suits agents: the specification is explicit and the failure is mechanical. An agent takes the decided rate and the property record, builds the submission in each platform's field set, validates it against the rules, and flags every field with no source rather than inventing a value.

Last room availability is a promise about nights you cannot see yet

Last room availability means the negotiated rate stays open whenever any room is open, including the nights you would otherwise sell at a premium. Agreeing to it is the quietest expensive decision of the season, usually taken in a hurry because the field is mandatory and the deadline is tomorrow.

An agent should not make that call. It should build the case for the person who does: what this account produced last year, which of those nights were compressed, what the house achieved on the nights the account booked and on the nights it did not, and where a static rate would have sat against the public rate across the year, which is the real argument between a fixed number and a dynamic discount.

Breakfast, parking and wifi, given away one tick box at a time

The amenity grid looks administrative. It is a price list. Breakfast, parking, wifi, lounge access, late checkout, an upgrade, loyalty points at a higher earn: each carries a cost per occupied room night, and together they move the effective rate further than the rate negotiation did. Group bids have the same problem under the name concessions, with complimentary rooms against the block, a rental waiver on the space, free audio visual and reduced corkage.

An agent can cost the requested package against the volume the buyer projects, then against the account's real production rather than its promised production.

The buyer still has the letter you sent them last year

Buyers and their agencies keep what you sent. An inconsistent bid invites a challenge: a rate structure that changed without explanation, an amenity withdrawn silently, a different answer to the same security question.

Account memory therefore matters more than library content. An agent keeps the history per account and per property, covering what you quoted, what was accepted and which concessions were granted. It drafts this year's bid against last year's and reports the differences plainly, so the person signing knows what the buyer will notice.

A group enquiry prices rooms, space and dinner in one answer

A meetings brief arrives as narrative, a platform form, or a document from a procurement team that has never run an event. Inside it are a room block with a night by night pattern, meeting space with a layout per session, catering with a minimum spend, a rooming list deadline and a cancellation schedule.

An agent reads that brief into a structured enquiry: pattern, space by session, catering, deadlines, terms proposed. It checks the pattern against inventory and the diary, prices the package from your own menus and rate structures, and drafts the proposal in the organiser's requested shape.

Attrition and cut off decide more of the profit than the rate

Attrition sets what the organiser pays if the block does not fill. The cut off date sets when unsold rooms come back to you, and whether they come back in time to be sold. An agent can extract the terms the buyer proposes, compare them against your standard contract line by line, and model what they mean across a range of pickup, so the risk is visible rather than theoretical.

The diary changed while the proposal was being written

The function diary is the fastest moving data in the building. Space is held and released. A residential group takes the whole floor and the breakout rooms become bedrooms. A proposal quoting space already gone is worse than one that arrives late, because it costs you the enquiry and the organiser's confidence.

Sequencing is something agents handle well. Availability is checked at drafting, rechecked immediately before the proposal goes out, and any conflict raised rather than resolved quietly. Placing or extending a hold stays a human action, because a hold displaces real revenue.

Behind the rate sheet, questions about insurance, access and carbon

Attached to the rate request, increasingly, is a questionnaire set with nothing to do with rooms. Public and employer liability cover with certificates. Fire risk assessment and evacuation procedure. Food safety rating and allergen controls. Modern slavery and labour practice. Sustainability certification, energy source, waste and water reporting, and a carbon figure per room night.

Those answers exist, but written for inspectors rather than buyers, and they sit behind dates. An agent retrieves the current approved answer, drafts it to the shape the buyer asked for, and carries the validity date of the source with it. Expiry is a blocking flag, not a footnote.

Accessibility answers describe the building, not the brand

How many accessible bedrooms, on which floors, with a roll in shower or a bath. Whether the lift reaches the function floor. Whether the plenary room has a hearing loop. An agent should draft from the property's own access statement and survey, and say plainly where no record exists rather than describing a feature nobody has verified.

The same account bids from several of your hotels at once

One corporate account can touch a dozen properties in your portfolio, each answered by a different revenue manager with a different view of demand. The buyer sees one submission and reads it as a portfolio position. Two of your hotels in the same city undercutting each other is visible to her and invisible to you.

An agent gives the group the consolidated view: which properties are bidding, the rate structure and amenity set each has proposed, where answers to the same questionnaire differ, and where a property has promised something the group has not agreed. The questionnaire is then answered once centrally and once per property for the parts that are genuinely local.

In spring, the account that promised volume and sent nobody

The season ends and the review does not happen, because the people who would do it have moved on. Accounts that projected substantial volume and delivered very little roll forward on the same rate and amenity package, year after year.

An agent can carry out the reconciliation nobody staffs. Match every negotiated agreement to production by account and property. Compare projected volume to what arrived, the negotiated rate to the rate achieved, and the cost of the concessions to the revenue generated. Flag bookings that came through other channels despite the agreement. The output is a list to reprice, drop or escalate before the next season opens.

Where AI fails here

The rate is not an agent's decision. Neither is last room availability, because it commits inventory you have not yet sold. Concessions, attrition terms, cut off dates and contract amendments all commit the property commercially, and each belongs to a named person who can be held to it. So does holding or releasing space. An agent that places a hold to make a proposal work has displaced revenue nobody authorised.

Judgement about the account sits outside the documents too. Whether to chase a piece of business, whether a buyer is testing the market or genuinely moving, what you signal by walking away: none of it is written down anywhere an agent can read.

The dangerous failures are the quiet ones. An agent that reuses a superseded capacity chart, an expired insurance certificate or a pre refurbishment room description produces exactly the misrepresentation you deployed it to avoid. Build it the other way round: every fact surfaced with its source and date, expiry treated as blocking, availability verified at the moment of drafting, and no commercial commitment entered without a human name against it.

Where to start

Do not begin by building a content library. Take last season's transient requests for one platform, or one month of group enquiries, and run the path again: requests reconciled against the accounts you expected, the property record assembled with a date against each value, an evidence pack built for every rate decision, concessions costed, bids drafted against what the same buyer was quoted last year, and a production review at the end. Two things usually become obvious: how much of your property data nobody can stand behind, and how much of the season went on retyping facts rather than deciding prices.
