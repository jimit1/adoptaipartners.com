AI for Compliance Evidence: Proving It Works When Risk or a Regulator Asks

28 August 2026  ·  7 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Most compliance teams do not have an insight problem. They have an assembly problem. Evidence for control attestations gets pulled by hand from ticketing systems, HR platforms, cloud consoles and shared drives. Control testing means sampling records one at a time and pasting screenshots into workpapers. When an auditor or a regulator asks how a decision was made, someone reconstructs the trail after the fact from emails and memory. The work is repetitive, deadline driven and painful to staff, and the output is only as defensible as the least careful person in the chain.

AI agents are well suited to this work precisely because it is assembly, not judgement, for most of its volume. The judgement sits in a minority of contested cases, and a well designed deployment routes exactly those cases to your people. Below are the use cases we see working inside enterprises, with enough detail that you can judge whether they fit your environment.

Recurring evidence collection for control attestations

The problem: every quarter, control owners are chased for the same artefacts. Access listings, change tickets, backup logs, training completions. Someone in compliance nags, collects, renames files and files them against the control library.

How it works: an agent holds a map of each control to its evidence sources. On schedule, it reads the source systems directly, the identity platform for access listings, the change management tool for tickets, the LMS for training records. For each control it decides whether the pulled artefact satisfies the evidence requirement as written, checking completeness, date range and population coverage. It writes the artefact back into your GRC platform against the correct control and period, with a generated cover note stating what was pulled, from where, when, and what the population count was.

The trail: every pull is logged with source, query, timestamp and a hash of the artefact, so an auditor can verify the evidence was not edited after collection. The handoff: any control whose evidence is missing, stale or short of the expected population goes to the control owner with a specific description of the gap, not a generic reminder.

Control testing on full populations instead of samples

The problem: manual testing forces sampling. Twenty five items out of thousands, because that is what a person can inspect in the time available. Sampling is a concession to labour cost, not a virtue.

How it works: an agent reads the full population for a control, for example every privileged access grant in the period, or every change deployed to production. For each item it applies the test steps as your methodology defines them: was the grant approved before provisioning, by someone with authority, with a ticket reference that matches. It decides pass, fail or unclear for each item and writes results into the testing workpaper with the item level rationale.

The trail: the workpaper shows every item tested, the attribute checked, the evidence relied on and the conclusion, which is a stronger position in front of an auditor than any sample. The handoff: fails and unclears go to a human tester with the specific attribute that failed. The person spends their time on exceptions, which is where their judgement matters.

Assembling responses to regulatory requests and exam letters

The problem: a request letter arrives with forty numbered items and a deadline. Someone builds a spreadsheet, assigns owners, chases, reviews, versions and packages. Weeks disappear.

How it works: an agent parses the letter into discrete asks and matches each one against your document repositories, policy library, prior exam responses and GRC records. It decides, for each item, whether responsive material already exists, partially exists or must be authored, and drafts a first response citing the specific internal documents it relied on. It writes the whole package into a tracker: item, status, source documents, draft response, assigned reviewer.

The trail: each draft answer carries its citations to internal sources, so a reviewer can verify the claim against the document in one click rather than trusting the summary. The handoff: nothing leaves the building without human sign off. The agent produces the assembled, cited draft. Your team, and usually counsel, reviews and approves every item. The agent's contribution is that review starts on day three instead of week three.

Keeping the policy to control map honest

The problem: regulations change, policies get revised, and the mapping between obligations, policies and controls quietly rots. The gap surfaces during an exam, which is the worst possible moment.

How it works: an agent reads your obligations register, your policy library and your control library, and continuously checks the threads between them. When a policy is revised, it decides whether the revision affects any mapped control, whether any obligation is now covered by weaker language, and whether a control's description still matches what the linked procedure actually says. It writes findings into your GRC tool as proposed mapping updates, never silent edits, each with the before text, the after text and the reason.

The trail: a versioned log of every proposed and accepted mapping change, which lets you show a regulator that coverage is actively maintained rather than asserted. The handoff: every proposed change is approved or rejected by the policy owner. The agent proposes, people dispose.

Quality assurance over alert handling and case decisions

The problem: second line QA over first line decisions, for example AML alert dispositions or complaints handling, is itself sampled thinly, so weak decisioning persists for months before anyone sees the pattern.

How it works: an agent reads closed cases in full: the alert, the analyst's narrative, the evidence attached and the disposition. It decides whether the narrative supports the disposition under your QA checklist, whether required steps were documented, and whether similar fact patterns received inconsistent outcomes. It writes a QA score with reasons against each case and a periodic pattern summary for the QA lead.

The trail: every scored case links the checklist item to the exact passage in the case record that satisfied or failed it. The handoff: cases scored as unsupported or inconsistent go to a human QA reviewer for final determination. The agent never reopens or overturns a case itself.

Measuring accuracy on contested cases before launch

Do not launch any of the above on the strength of a demo. The demo will show the easy cases, and easy cases were never your problem.

Before go live, build an evaluation set from your own history, weighted towards the hard cases: the control tests where two reviewers disagreed, the exam items that took three drafts, the QA calls that were escalated. A few hundred cases with known, argued over outcomes. Run the agent against that set and measure agreement with the settled human answer, separately for the easy majority and the contested minority. The contested number is the one that matters, and it is the one that decides where the confidence threshold sits for automatic handling versus human referral.

Then keep measuring after launch. Route a fixed percentage of the agent's confident answers to human review anyway, blind, and track drift. When source systems, policies or regulations change, rerun the evaluation set before trusting the agent's output in the new world. This is the same discipline you would apply to a new hire in a controlled function, and the agent should not be exempt from it.

Where to start

Start with the process that is highest volume, lowest ambiguity and already well documented, which for most organisations is recurring evidence collection or full population testing of one or two mechanical controls. Choose something where the ground truth is checkable, so your first months produce a measurable accuracy record rather than a debate.

Write down the current process honestly, including the informal steps people do from memory, because the agent will be built against that description. Involve internal audit early, not at the end. If your auditors help define the trail the agent must produce, they will accept its output far more readily. Expect the first deployment to take weeks of iteration against your evaluation set before it earns unattended operation on any subset of cases, and treat that period as control design, not delay.

Where AI fails here

Honesty matters more in this domain than most, so here is where these systems fall short. Agents fail when the underlying records are wrong, because they will faithfully assemble bad data into a well formatted trail. They fail on genuinely novel judgement, the first case of a new typology, the regulation with no interpretive history, and they must be configured to recognise novelty and refer it rather than improvise. They can misread ambiguous policy language with complete confidence, which is why contested case testing and citation to source are non negotiable. And they cannot own an outcome. Accountability to a regulator sits with named humans in your organisation, and no deployment we would build changes that. The agent compresses the assembly work and widens the coverage. The accountable judgement stays with you.

Done well, this is not a shortcut around your control framework. It is a way to run the framework on full populations, with trails that are generated at the moment of the work rather than reconstructed under exam pressure, and with your specialists spending their hours on the cases that genuinely need them.
