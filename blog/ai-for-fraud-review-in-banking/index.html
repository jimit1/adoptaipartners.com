AI for fraud review in banking: the queue behind the detection engine

19 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A fraud analyst opens an alert. The reason code says velocity anomaly on a card not present transaction. To decide anything, they open the core banking screen for balance and recent history, the card processor portal for the authorisation trail, the case system for whether this customer has been here before, the device and session log if the channel team has given them access, and the KYC record to remember who the customer actually is. Then they call the customer, who does not answer. Then they write it up.

Six systems, one decision, and most of those lookups produce nothing that changes the outcome. That is the shape of fraud review in most banks, and it is why the queue is the constraint rather than the model.

The detection layer is not the problem

Banks have spent years on transaction monitoring, scoring engines and rules. Those systems work well enough to generate far more alerts than the team can clear properly. Tuning down to reduce volume means missing fraud, and nobody wants to defend that after a loss. So the thresholds stay and the burden lands on people.

The result is familiar. A large queue, most of it benign, cleared under a service level that rewards speed. Analysts develop shortcuts that are rational individually and dangerous in aggregate. Cases get closed with a two line note that nobody could reconstruct six months later when the customer complains to the ombudsman.

An agent placed here does not decide better than your model. It removes the retrieval and assembly work that sits between the alert and the analyst's judgement.

Triage and enrichment before anyone opens the case

The highest value work happens before a human sees the alert. For each item in the queue, an agent can pull the context an analyst would have pulled anyway and attach it to the case.

That means the account's recent behaviour in plain terms rather than raw rows: usual channels, usual counterparties, usual value bands, and exactly how this activity departs from them. It means the authorisation detail from the card processor, including whether strong customer authentication was applied, whether the transaction was stepped up, and what the issuer response was. It means prior alerts and prior cases on the same customer, the same device, the same beneficiary and the same merchant, which is the single most useful piece of context and the one analysts skip most often under time pressure.

It also means clearing the obvious. A card alert on a customer who logged a travel notification, or a payment matching a documented standing arrangement, should arrive pre annotated with the reason, so the analyst confirms rather than researches.

The framing that keeps this safe is that enrichment is not disposition. The agent reorders and explains the queue. Closing an alert as no fraud remains an action taken by a named person under your policy.

Disputes and chargebacks

Card dispute intake is a paperwork problem wearing a fraud costume. A customer reports a transaction they do not recognise. To progress it through the scheme process you need the transaction reference, the merchant descriptor as it appeared to the customer, the dispute reason mapped to the correct scheme condition, the date of first contact, and whatever supporting material the reason code requires.

Most of that arrives incomplete through a contact centre, and the case bounces back and forth while someone chases it. An agent can read the customer's own words from the call summary or the secure message, map them to the probable scheme reason, retrieve the transaction and the merchant record, and identify precisely what is missing before the case reaches the disputes team. It can also flag the disputes that are not fraud at all, the merchant service disputes, the free trial that converted, the family member with the card, which follow a different path entirely.

Provisional credit decisions, representment strategy and any first party fraud suspicion stay with the team.

Authorised push payment and social engineering cases

These are the hardest cases to work and the hardest to write up. The payment was authorised by the customer, the credentials were genuine, and the question is whether the customer was deceived, how the deception ran, and what the bank did or failed to do at the point of payment.

The evidence is scattered and partly narrative. You need the payment instruction and its timing, the confirmation of payee result and whether it was a match, a close match or no match, the warning shown to the customer and whether they clicked through it, the session history showing how quickly the payment was set up, prior payments to the same beneficiary, and the customer's account of what happened, which usually arrives as an unstructured statement.

An agent can assemble that into a chronology with each element sourced, and compare the customer's account against what the systems show, surfacing discrepancies rather than resolving them. It can classify the apparent typology, impersonation of the bank, invoice redirection, romance, investment, purchase scam, which matters because reimbursement assessment and reporting both turn on it.

What it must not do is form a view on customer liability. Whether the customer met the standard of caution expected of them carries reimbursement, complaint and ombudsman consequences. It is a decision of record, and it stays with an assessor.

Mule accounts and the receiving side

Mule investigation is where the queue mindset breaks down, because the unit of work is a network rather than an alert. A beneficiary receiving funds from several unrelated victims is visible only across cases, never within one.

An agent is useful here for the joining work. Given a beneficiary, it can retrieve every inbound credit flagged in fraud or reported by another institution, the onward movement and its speed, the account opening record, the device and address overlaps with other accounts, and the account's behaviour since opening against the profile stated at onboarding. Newly opened, dormant then suddenly active, immediate onward transfer to multiple destinations: recognisable patterns, and an agent can put the supporting data on one page.

Account restriction, exit and reporting decisions belong to the financial crime team. Restricting a customer's account wrongly is a serious harm and needs a human who can be asked to explain it.

Sanctions and AML alerts as adjacent work

Most fraud teams sit near a financial crime operations function working sanctions screening hits and transaction monitoring alerts, and the review problem is similar enough that the same tooling usually applies.

Screening hits are mostly name similarity noise. An agent can lay out the matched attributes next to the customer record, date of birth, nationality, address, known identifiers, and state which agree, which conflict and which are absent, so the analyst adjudicates on a comparison rather than a fuzzy match score. AML alerts benefit from the same enrichment as fraud alerts: expected activity from the customer profile, actual activity, and the specific gap between them.

The discipline is identical. The agent presents; the analyst adjudicates; the decision carries a name.

Evidence gathering and narrative drafting

The write up is the part analysts hate and the part that matters most later. A suspicious activity report or suspicious transaction report narrative has to state who, what, when, where and why it is suspicious, in prose, with the underlying activity described accurately.

An agent drafting from the case file produces a first version with the transactions summarised correctly, the chronology in order, the account and party details pulled from source rather than retyped, and the reason for suspicion stated in the analyst's own framing. Editing that is a far faster task than composing from a blank screen while switching between six systems.

Two constraints are absolute. The narrative must contain nothing the case evidence does not support, so every factual assertion should carry a pointer back to its source and anything unsupported should be visibly marked rather than smoothed over. And the decision to file is not the agent's. The nominated officer or money laundering reporting officer owns the filing decision under your regime, personally, and an agent that drafts well makes that role easier rather than smaller.

Quality assurance and model governance

Quality assurance improves when the sample stops being random. An agent can read closed cases against your own review standard and surface the ones worth a second look: decisions closed faster than the evidence could have been read, rationales that do not reference the alert reason, cases closed as no fraud where a later chargeback or report contradicts the outcome, and analysts whose patterns drift from their peers on similar alert types.

Your regulator and your model risk function will ask about the agent itself. Expect to document the intended use narrowly, version the prompts, retrieval configuration and underlying model as change controlled items, retain inputs and outputs for every case so a decision can be reconstructed, and evidence that the agent does not produce disparate treatment across customer groups. The strongest governance argument is scope: an agent that retrieves, summarises and drafts for a named decision maker is a far smaller exercise than one that disposes of alerts.

Where AI fails here

Bad source data becomes confident prose. If the card processor feed lags, if the beneficiary name is truncated, if the KYC record is stale, the agent assembles a consistent case file around the wrong facts. A tidy narrative is more persuasive than a messy one, which is exactly the risk.

Retrieval gaps are silent. Device and session data often sits with a channel team on a different retention schedule. If a lookup returns nothing, the case reads as though nothing happened rather than as though nothing was found, and analysts will not notice unless the absence is stated explicitly.

Novel typologies are invisible to it. An agent reasons from patterns in your existing data and your written standards. The first wave of a new scam looks like ordinary activity, and your best analysts spot it because something felt wrong. That does not survive summarisation.

Automation complacency is the real operational risk. Once the drafts are usually right, reviewers stop reading properly, and the one case where the summary was wrong is the one that reaches a complaint or a regulator. Build sampling that assumes this will happen.

Nothing here moves accountability. Filing decisions, reimbursement outcomes, account restrictions and exits sit with named individuals under your regime, and an investigator assistant does not change that.
