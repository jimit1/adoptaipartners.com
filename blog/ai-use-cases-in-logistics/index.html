AI Use Cases in Logistics: The Admin Behind Every Load

3 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A logistics business is judged on whether the vehicle arrives. It is paid on whether the paperwork behind the vehicle holds up. Between those two facts sits a layer of administration nobody designed: bookings arriving in six formats, delivery notes photographed in a yard, carrier invoices checked by whoever has time, and a customer service team answering the same question for the fourth time.

That layer is where AI is genuinely useful here, and not where the noise points. Route optimisation and forecasting get the attention, and already live inside the systems you bought.

What follows is a map rather than a deep dive. For each area the question is the same three part one: what does the agent actually do, which system does it write into, and what does a human still own. If a supplier cannot answer the third part, the first two are not worth much.

Booking intake and shipment data capture

Most operators have a customer portal and almost none of their customers use it. Orders arrive as an email with a table pasted into the body, a spreadsheet with merged cells and a header row that moves, a scanned purchase order, and an EDI feed that works perfectly for the three customers who have it.

An agent reads whatever arrived, identifies the consignments, and proposes a booking in your transport management system: addresses matched to your location records, dates, pallet counts, weights, any temperature or tail lift requirement, and the references the customer will later quote back at you. Each value stays linked to its place in the source, so a planner verifies in seconds rather than re-reading the email. Accepting work outside normal service stays a commercial decision, and the planner owns the match to a vehicle and slot.

The customer whose spreadsheet changes every week

Templates per customer rot, so the agent has to read structure rather than fixed cell positions, and flag what it could not read confidently instead of booking a guess.

Where cash gets stuck behind a signed note

Proof of delivery is where cash gets stuck. The driver returns a CMR note or delivery note with a signature, a time, sometimes a pallet count amended in biro, occasionally a note about damage nobody keys anywhere.

An agent matches each document or image to the consignment, reads the signed quantity, time and signatory, compares them against plan, and flags what does not agree: short deliveries, refusals, unsigned notes, clausing, pallet counts that will cause an equipment dispute later. It writes the confirmed delivery and attaches the evidence in your transport management system so invoicing can run, and keeps a queue of missing proof of delivery by customer and driver. Humans own the disputes; the agent ensures none are found six weeks later by a credit controller.

Carrier invoice and freight bill checking

If you subcontract, you are invoiced on terms you negotiated and nobody has time to verify line by line. Accessorial charges are where the leakage lives: waiting time, re-delivery, out of hours, pallet exchange shortfalls, fuel surcharge on the wrong base, a tail lift charge on a vehicle that did not need one.

An agent matches every invoice line to the consignment and the rate agreement, recalculates from the contracted basis, and sorts the invoice into lines that agree, lines that disagree with a stated figure and reason, and lines it cannot evidence either way. For a disputed line it drafts the query with the consignment reference, the contracted rate and the timestamps supporting your position. Approved lines go through to your finance system and the rest are held. Nothing is paid on the agent's authority, and letting a small charge go stays your people's call.

Exceptions, delays and status that reaches the customer

An exception is only expensive when it is found late. The agent's role is monitoring: comparing planned against actual across telematics, driver app events, subcontractor portals and inbound EDI 214 status messages, then detecting the load that has not moved, has missed a window, or will not make its appointment.

It then does the administration the exception creates: raising it on the consignment with a reason code, drafting the notification in the format that customer expects, proposing a recovery option, and recording the cause so the monthly review has data rather than anecdote. Choosing the recovery stays a planner's call.

Track and trace enquiries

A large share of your customer service volume is one question: where is it.

An agent looks the consignment up across the transport management system, the warehouse system and the subcontractor's data, then drafts a specific answer with the reference, the last confirmed event and the expected window, attaching the signed proof of delivery where the question asked for it. It should also recognise the enquiry that is really a claim or a credit request and route it with the record already assembled. Keep a person on any customer in escalation, and keep the agent in draft mode until you have seen its answers on your own awkward cases.

Dock slots, driver scheduling and the yard

Warehouse operators spend real hours on booking in. Carriers request slots by email and phone, drivers arrive outside their window, and the labour on shift does not match the work booked against it.

An agent takes slot requests in whatever form they arrive, checks them against dock capacity, opening hours, equipment and planned labour, and writes the confirmed slot into the warehouse management system or booking tool. It sends the confirmation, chases carriers who have not booked against an expected inbound, and re-proposes when arrivals slip. The same pattern covers driver scheduling: checking a plan against drivers' hours rules, licence and training records and vehicle availability, and flagging the conflict before the shift rather than at the gate. The agent proposes and transport managers approve, because a drivers' hours decision is theirs in law.

Claims for loss and damage

A claim usually starts as an email that does not say it is a claim. An agent catches it early, opens the claim record and assembles the file: the consignment, the signed delivery note and any clausing on it, photographs, temperature trace where relevant, the subcontractor involved, and the terms the movement travelled under with the liability limit and deadlines that follow.

It then states a position: what is evidenced, what is missing, the probable liability band, and whether a recovery claim against a subcontractor or insurer should be lodged, with that notification drafted. Accepting liability and settling stay with a named person.

Regulated movements and the paperwork that proves them

The documentation around a regulated movement is checkable work. An agent can verify that a dangerous goods note carries a consistent proper shipping name, class, packing group and quantity, that segregation rules are not breached by the other goods on the load, and that waste transfer or food safety paperwork exists where the commodity requires it. It can keep the audit trail an operator licence review will ask for and chase the expired certificate before it stops a load.

What it does not do is sign. The dangerous goods safety adviser's judgement, the declaration and the acceptance of a consignment for carriage belong to a qualified, named human.

Rate requests and tender responses

Rate requests arrive as a spreadsheet of lanes with a deadline. Completing one is hours of lookup: has this lane run before, at what cost, what do subcontractor rates say now, which lanes have no credible basis.

An agent assembles the evidence per lane, prefills the rate tender workbook in the customer's format, and flags the lanes it has nothing reliable to price from. Pricing stays a commercial act; the value is that your pricing people spend the time on lanes that matter rather than on retrieval.

Where AI fails here

Master data sets the ceiling on all of it. If the same delivery site exists four times under four names, an agent will match one of them and your reporting stays wrong. If rate agreements live in email threads rather than a rate table, invoice audit cannot work, because there is nothing to audit against. That is a data project, and anyone saying an agent fixes it alone is selling.

Exception taxonomies are the second trap. Most operators have reason codes that overlap, mean different things at different depots, and include a catch all absorbing much of everything. An agent classifies consistently against whatever definitions you give it, which mostly exposes that the definitions were never agreed.

Documents that are photographs of paper remain genuinely hard. A creased delivery note shot in a dark yard, with a stamp over the quantity and a biro amendment in the margin, will defeat extraction some of the time. The right behaviour is to queue it, not to produce a confident number. Every deployment needs that queue and a named person who drains it daily.

Hallucinated reconciliation is worth stating plainly. An agent asked to match an invoice to a rate will, if pushed, produce a tidy explanation for a figure it cannot evidence, and that is worse than no reconciliation because it looks finished. Require a citation for every matched line and treat an unevidenced one as an exception, not a pass.

Then there is asymmetry. A correct automated booking saves a planner two minutes; a wrong one sends a vehicle to the wrong site. The economics depend on the cost of being wrong, not the time saved, which is why low consequence work automates first.

Last, and most common: an agent cannot operate a process nobody has written down. If three planners handle the same exception three defensible ways, there is no rule to encode. Agreeing the rule is yours, and we will say so when that is what we find.

Where to start

Pick one process, narrow, high volume and measurable. For most road and warehousing operators that is proof of delivery capture and reconciliation: daily, the documents are yours, the target is unambiguous, and the benefit shows up as days off the invoicing cycle. Carrier invoice audit is the other common first choice, where rate data is already in reasonable shape.

Run it in draft mode, with a person reviewing every output, until you have an accuracy figure measured on your own difficult documents. Then widen the autonomy rules one at a time, starting where being wrong is cheap.
