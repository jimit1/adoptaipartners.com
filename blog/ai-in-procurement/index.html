AI in procurement: from requisition to three-way match

28 August 2026  ·  6 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Procurement teams sit between everyone who wants to buy something and every rule about how buying is allowed to happen. The result is a queue: requisitions with missing cost centres, purchase orders raised after the invoice arrived, suppliers onboarded twice under slightly different names, contracts renewing silently because nobody had the renewal date in a calendar, and a three-way match process that generates hundreds of exceptions a month, most of which turn out to be a unit-of-measure mismatch or a freight line nobody expected. The work is not intellectually hard. It is relentless, detailed and scattered across an ERP, a contract repository, a supplier portal and several inboxes. That combination, high volume, clear rules, messy inputs, multiple systems, is precisely where production AI agents pay for themselves fastest.

Requisition intake and first-pass validation

Most bad purchase orders start as bad requisitions. An agent at the front of the process reads each incoming requisition, whether it arrives through the ERP form, an email to the procurement inbox or a ticket, and validates it before a buyer ever sees it. It checks the cost centre exists and is open, the requested item maps to a catalogue entry or an existing contract, the amount sits inside the requester's delegation of authority, and the description is specific enough to buy against.

Where something is wrong or vague, the agent drafts the query back to the requester, naming exactly what is missing, and holds the requisition in a waiting state rather than letting it clog the buyer queue. Where everything checks out and the item is on an existing contract or catalogue, it prepares the purchase order in the ERP for one-click release, or releases it automatically if you have chosen to allow that below a value threshold. Buyers stop being data-entry clerks and start seeing only the requisitions that need sourcing judgement.

Guided buying and contract awareness

A surprising share of maverick spend is not rebellion, it is ignorance. The requester did not know a contract existed. An agent with read access to your contract repository can fix this at the moment of intake: when a requisition for laptops, temporary labour or logistics services arrives, it checks whether a negotiated agreement covers the category, compares the requested price against contracted rates, and either routes the purchase onto the contract or flags the delta.

When someone tries to buy off-contract at a worse price, the agent does not block them, it annotates the requisition with the contracted alternative and the price difference, and routes it to the buyer with that context. Compliance improves because the compliant path became the easy path, not because anyone wrote a sterner policy document.

Supplier onboarding and master data hygiene

Supplier master data is where fraud hides and duplicate payments breed. An agent can run the onboarding checklist end to end: collect the supplier's registration details, validate the tax identifier against the official format for the jurisdiction, check the bank account details follow the right structure, screen the name against your sanctions and watchlist tooling, and search the existing vendor master for near-duplicates, the same company under "Ltd" and "Limited", the same bank account under two names.

It writes the assembled, validated record into the ERP as a pending vendor and routes it for human approval with a summary of every check and its result. Bank detail changes on existing suppliers, the classic invoice fraud vector, get special handling: the agent never applies them directly, it verifies the request against the known contact on file, flags mismatches loudly and queues the change for a human with dual approval. The agent does the legwork; a person owns the moment of trust.

The three-way match, and the exceptions that eat your team

Matching purchase order, goods receipt and invoice is mechanical when everything agrees, and your ERP already automates that happy path. The real cost is the exception queue. An agent working that queue reads each mismatch and diagnoses it the way your best accounts payable analyst would. Quantity mismatch: was there a partial delivery, and does a second receipt complete the picture? Price mismatch: is it within tolerance, is there a contract price update the PO missed, is it a currency rounding artefact? Extra line: freight, surcharge or genuinely unordered goods?

For each exception it writes its diagnosis and evidence into the case, resolves the categories you have authorised it to resolve, tolerance-level price variances, matched partial deliveries, and drafts the supplier query or the internal query for everything else. A human clears the remainder from a queue where every item arrives pre-investigated. Teams typically find the majority of exceptions were never disputes at all, just missing context that an agent can assemble in seconds.

Contract renewals and expiry watch

Auto-renewal is a tax on inattention. An agent that reads your contract repository maintains a live view of every renewal date, notice period and price adjustment clause. Ninety days before a notice deadline it opens a renewal case: current spend against the contract pulled from the ERP, service issues logged against the supplier, the market alternatives you already have on file, and a recommendation to renew, renegotiate or run a sourcing event.

It drafts the notice letter when you decide to exit, and the calendar of obligations either way. Nothing renews silently, and negotiations start with the leverage of time rather than the desperation of a deadline that passed last Tuesday.

Spend visibility without the quarterly archaeology

Most spend analysis is a quarterly project because classification is manual. An agent classifies transactions continuously as they post, mapping supplier and line description to your category taxonomy, flagging the ones it cannot classify confidently for human decision, and learning from each correction. The output is not a dashboard for its own sake, it is answers on demand: what did we spend with this supplier across all entities, where is tail spend concentrating, which categories drifted off contract this quarter. Category managers get current data when negotiating, not last quarter's.

Where to start

Start with the three-way match exception queue or requisition validation, not with sourcing. Both have unambiguous ground truth, the documents either reconcile or they do not, high volume, and a measurable baseline: exceptions cleared per person per day, requisition cycle time. Run the agent in draft mode for the first month, where it diagnoses and proposes but a person applies, and track its accuracy per exception category. Promote categories to autonomous handling one at a time as the accuracy earns it. Supplier onboarding is a strong second move once you trust the pattern, and contract renewal watch is a quiet win that needs almost no process change. Save negotiation support and category strategy for later; they benefit from AI, but they are judgement work layered on data the earlier agents will have cleaned up.

Where AI fails here

An honest list. Agents inherit the quality of your master data, and procurement master data is usually poor. Expect the first weeks to surface an uncomfortable backlog of duplicate vendors, dead catalogue entries and contracts nobody filed, which is valuable, but it is cleanup work someone must own. Agents are weak where the ground truth is genuinely contested: a price mismatch rooted in a verbal agreement between a buyer and a sales rep leaves no document trail to read, and the agent will diagnose it wrongly or not at all.

Negotiation itself should stay human. An agent can brief you brilliantly and model scenarios, but supplier relationships are built on judgement, timing and trust that a system should support, not simulate. Fraud is an arms race: an agent catches the crude bank-change scams, and its existence will push attackers towards more convincing forgeries, so keep the dual human approval on payment details permanently. Finally, beware silent policy drift in the other direction, if the agent auto-approves within tolerances, review those tolerances periodically, because suppliers learn thresholds faster than you would like.

Procurement is one of the few functions where the business case for agents is usually visible within a quarter, because the baseline metrics already exist and the exceptions are already counted. The pattern is consistent: agents read everything, resolve the mechanical majority, and hand humans a smaller queue of genuinely interesting problems with the evidence attached.
