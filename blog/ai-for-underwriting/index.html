AI for underwriting: decisions with an audit trail

28 August 2026  ·  6 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Underwriting teams are drowning in submissions they never asked for. Brokers send risk in every format imaginable, half of it incomplete, and your underwriters spend their mornings rekeying ACORD forms, chasing missing loss runs and triaging a queue that grew overnight. The work that actually needs judgement, pricing a genuinely marginal risk, gets squeezed into whatever hours are left. Meanwhile every declined submission, every referral and every exception must be defensible to auditors, reinsurers and regulators. Speed without an audit trail is not an option in this business, and an audit trail without speed loses you the broker.

This is exactly the shape of problem that production AI agents handle well, provided they are built to record why they did what they did. Here is where the work actually lands.

Submission intake that reads like an underwriter

The first agent most carriers deploy sits at the front door. It reads the broker email, the attached ACORD forms, the schedules of values, the loss runs and the supplementary questionnaires, whether they arrive as PDFs, spreadsheets or photographed paper. It extracts the named insured, locations, limits, class codes and requested effective dates, then writes a structured submission record into your policy administration system or underwriting workbench.

Crucially, it does not stop at extraction. It checks the submission against your appetite guide. Is this class of business one you write? Is the total insured value inside authority? Is the state licensed? Out-of-appetite submissions get a drafted decline for an underwriter to review and release, with the appetite rule that triggered it cited in the file. In-appetite submissions get created, enriched and queued. What used to take forty minutes of rekeying takes minutes, and every field the agent populated carries a pointer back to the source document and page.

Completeness checks and broker chase

Incomplete submissions are the silent killer of quote turnaround. An agent can hold each submission against a checklist derived from your underwriting guidelines for that line and class: five years of loss runs for this size of risk, a signed supplemental for this hazard class, currently valued experience within ninety days.

When something is missing, the agent drafts the chase email to the broker listing exactly what is outstanding, in your house style, for the underwriter or assistant to review and send. It logs the request in the submission record, watches the inbox for the reply, attaches the returned documents, re-runs the completeness check and updates the status. Underwriters see a queue where every submission is either ready to work or clearly marked with what it is waiting for and since when. Nothing goes stale in silence.

Risk triage and referral routing

Not every submission deserves the same underwriter. An agent can score each complete submission against your triage rules: hit ratio history with that broker, class hazard grade, size relative to authority levels, catastrophe zone exposure, prior declinatures on the same insured. It then routes the file, straightforward renewals within authority to the standard queue, complex or high-hazard risks to a senior underwriter, and anything touching a referral trigger to the referral queue with a pre-written summary of why.

The summary matters as much as the routing. Instead of opening a raw file, the referral underwriter opens a one-page brief: what the risk is, what triggered the referral, what the guidelines say, what similar risks on the book look like. The agent recommends, the human decides, and the decision plus rationale is written back into the system of record. That is the division of labour that keeps regulators comfortable and underwriters in charge.

Pre-fill and sanity checks on rating

Rating engines are only as good as what gets typed into them. An agent that has already read the submission can pre-populate the rating worksheet, flag inconsistencies before they become mispriced policies, a building value that fell 40 percent from expiring with no explanation, a payroll figure that disagrees between the ACORD and the supplemental, a protection class that does not match the address.

The agent does not set the price. It prepares the inputs, annotates anything anomalous, and drafts the quote document once the underwriter has settled terms. Where you have schedule rating credits or debits, it checks the justification text is present and consistent with what the file evidences, because that is precisely the field a market conduct exam will read.

Renewal preparation months before the date

Renewals are predictable and still somehow always late. An agent working your renewal book starts ninety or one hundred and twenty days out. It pulls the expiring policy, current loss experience, any midterm endorsements and open claims, then assembles a renewal file with a change summary: exposure movement, loss ratio for the term, claims still open and their reserves, any new locations or vehicles that appeared midterm.

For clean renewals inside authority it drafts the renewal quote for review. For anything that has deteriorated it flags the file early with what changed, so the underwriter has time for a real conversation with the broker rather than a last-minute scramble. Every renewal decision, including the ones the agent teed up and the human approved unchanged, is logged with its evidence.

The audit trail as a first-class output

Most vendors treat logging as plumbing. In underwriting it is the product. Every agent action should write a durable record: the documents it read, the fields it extracted with source references, the rules it evaluated and their outcomes, the recommendation it made, the human who accepted or overrode it, and when. Overrides deserve special care, since the pattern of overrides is how you improve both the guidelines and the agent.

Done properly, this gives you something your current process almost certainly lacks: a complete, queryable history of how every risk was handled. Reinsurance audits, regulator queries and internal QA stop being archaeology. You can answer "why did we quote this" or "why did we decline that" in seconds, with evidence, for any file in the book.

Where to start

Start at intake, not at pricing. Submission ingestion and completeness checking are high volume, low controversy and easy to measure, quote turnaround time and rekeying hours drop within weeks, and no one worries about a machine reading a PDF. Pick one line of business with decent volume and reasonably documented appetite and guidelines. Run the agent in draft mode first, where it prepares everything and a human releases everything, and measure the override rate. As trust builds, let clearly in-appetite, complete submissions flow straight to the underwriter queue without a review step. Only after intake is boringly reliable should you extend into triage scoring and renewal preparation. Resist the temptation to start with an agent that recommends price. That is the hardest problem, the most regulated surface and the slowest place to earn trust.

Where AI fails here

Be honest about the edges. Agents misread genuinely ambiguous documents, a loss run with inconsistent claim numbering, a schedule of values where the broker merged cells creatively, and if you let extraction errors flow silently into rating you will misprice risk at scale. Confidence thresholds and human review on low-confidence extractions are not optional.

Agents also cannot exercise underwriting judgement, and pretending otherwise is how carriers get hurt. The decision to stretch for a strategic broker, to read a deteriorating risk as a turnaround story, to decline a technically acceptable account on gut feel earned over twenty years, that stays human. An agent trained on your historical decisions will also faithfully learn your historical biases, so if your book has been redlining a class of business by accident, the agent will automate that mistake unless you audit for it. And guidelines drift: when appetite changes, someone must own updating the rules the agent runs on, or it will keep enforcing last year's strategy with perfect consistency.

None of this argues against deployment. It argues for deploying with review gates, override logging and a named owner for the rulebook.

Underwriting is a decision factory, and factories run better when the material handling is automated and the craftsmen are freed to do the work only they can do. The carriers getting this right are not replacing underwriters, they are giving each one the throughput of three and a file history an auditor can love.
