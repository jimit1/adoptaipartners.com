AI for Fraud and Abuse Review in Technology: The Queue Behind the Score

13 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

If you run trust and safety, payments risk or the abuse queue at a software company, you already have the detection layer: rules at signup, a vendor score on the card, a blocklist that has grown for years, alerts into a channel somebody watches. That is not the hard part.

The hard part is the queue those controls produce. Your product delivers value in seconds, with no shipment to intercept and no settlement delay, and the abuse arrives looking like your best customers: bulk seat purchases, sustained compute, logins from everywhere, a procurement team that appeared yesterday. The evidence that separates them sits across billing, the audit log, the product and a support thread.

The procurement card that looks exactly like a stolen one

An enterprise buyer's first self serve touch trips every rule you own. A brand new corporate mailbox, a card issued to a parent entity in another country, and seats bought in a block minutes after signup by someone in finance who has never opened the product.

A velocity rule cannot separate that from a stolen instrument being spent fast, and the two errors do not cost the same. Letting a small fraud through costs some compute and a dispute fee, and you find out. Declining a genuine buyer costs a deal you never learn you lost. The wrong accept is bounded; the wrong decline is unbounded and invisible.

Signup, card and the first hour of usage

The free tier is an attack surface rather than a marketing choice, and your billing endpoint is itself a product: it will be used to test whether cards are live. The familiar signals apply and you collect them already: subaddressed mailboxes, reused device fingerprints, signup bursts out of one address range.

What is specific to software is that the decision point is provisioning, not payment. An invited colleague, a document read and a configuration that took effort look nothing like a tenant that minted an access key within a minute and started a job. An agent reading that first hour alongside the payment attempt produces one recommendation with both halves visible, while reversible friction is still cheap.

Compute abuse on trial infrastructure

Mining, scraping farms, proxy relays, bulk mail through your workers, build minutes burned on somebody else's workload. This is not fraud against a card but a direct cost of goods problem, and it scales the moment one actor finds the gap.

The signature sits in the shape of usage rather than any single event: processing pinned at the ceiling, no interactive sessions, outbound connections to pools, workloads that restart the instant they are killed, job definitions identical across unrelated tenants.

A real customer running a genuine batch looks similar from a distance, so what is worth building is a ranked case with the distinguishing features written out, plus a drafted throttle decision. Throttling is reversible. Terminating a research team's tenant is not.

When a dozen workspaces are one person

Multi accounting is the structural abuse of a self serve product: one actor runs many tenants to keep harvesting the free tier, to stay under per account limits, or to return after a ban.

The links exist: instrument fingerprint, device signals, phone, signup range, reused keys, the same connected repository, the same webhook destination, recognisable phrasing in tickets. The failure mode is over-linking, because shared infrastructure is everywhere: an office gateway or a popular hosting provider merges strangers into one cluster if edge count stands in for evidence.

Writing down why this looks like the same actor

Here a language model beats a graph tool. The reviewer does not need a cluster diagram; they need a paragraph saying which edges carry weight, which are ambient, and whether any member carries a prior decision this tenant is evading. A written hypothesis can be argued with. A score cannot.

The credit that was meant to buy a first project

Promotional credit, referral bonuses, startup programmes and partner grants all have terms, and most abuse of them is a plain mismatch nobody checks at redemption. Referral rings where both sides are the same person. A code meant for one conference that ends up on a deals forum as "TRIAL90". Programme credit applied to accounts that were never new.

An agent holding those terms as written, reading redemptions against them and drafting both the clawback and the note to the owner removes a loss that sits with nobody.

A login from a new country, or a founder on a plane

Takeover rarely begins with a password. It begins with a token lifted from a machine, a key committed to a public repository, an authorised application with scopes nobody reviewed, or a support agent talked into changing a mailbox.

Location tells you almost nothing, because customers travel and attackers rent addresses anywhere. The sequence after the login tells you a great deal. A traveller reads and replies. A takeover changes the notification address, mints a broadly scoped key, invites an external member, exports data and disables the alerting that would have said so.

An agent watching that sequence in order, against the account's own history, beats any geography rule, and it can settle which contact route is still trustworthy once the mailbox on file may belong to the attacker.

Orders that arrive through a reseller

Channel business reintroduces older problems. Licences and credits are provisioned on invoice terms before money moves, so an accepted order is value already delivered. A partner passes on an order from an end customer who does not exist, or who never asked. Remittance details get altered in transit.

These checks are documentary rather than behavioural: the order against the partner agreement, the discount against that tier's entitlement, the end customer against your own records, the banking detail against what you held before. Document comparison, which is what quarter end squeezes out.

Disputes on a subscription worth less than the argument

On a low value monthly subscription the economics of representment are upside down. The fee and the handling time exceed the charge, so teams refund everything, the ratio your acquirer watches keeps climbing, and the same cardholders dispute again next month.

What makes representment viable is assembly cost, not legal skill. The pack is always the same: acceptance of terms with a timestamp, signup address and device, usage logs showing the service was consumed, invoices and prior payments, and the cancellation path as presented. Many of these disputes are not fraud at all but a statement line nobody recognised, reading something like "CLOUD PLATFORM 7".

An agent that builds the pack and flags where the descriptor or the cancellation flow generates disputes makes the process worth running again. It also sets the clock for anything that learns: outcomes land after the dispute window closes, so your labels always describe an older adversary.

What a reviewer needs from the audit log, the ledger and the API keys

The highest return change in most abuse operations is not a better model. It is that a reviewer opens a case and the file is already built, in the same shape every time: signup provenance, instrument history including failures elsewhere, the billing ledger and credit grants, product telemetry, the administrative audit log, the support thread, and linked accounts with their prior decisions.

The sentence that says what would change the decision

Add the thing most case files lack. State plainly what evidence would flip the recommendation: a verified instrument, a corporate identity, a reply from the registered owner through a trusted channel. That tells the reviewer what to ask for, and if the account appeals it is already the test you apply.

Ranking the queue by what a wrong answer costs

Order the queue by the expected cost of being wrong rather than by score, so an unclear case on an account with enterprise characteristics outranks an obvious scraping tenant.

Then separate actions by reversibility and let automation operate only on the undoable rungs: step up verification, a spend ceiling, a provisioning hold, a re-authorisation request. Anything that destroys state or ends a relationship stays with a person.

The appeal arrives from someone who was right

Most appeals against abuse decisions come from legitimate users, because that is who writes in. A working route needs the original evidence retrievable, a reviewer who did not make the first call, and a turnaround short enough that the customer has not already migrated.

An agent can assemble the original file plus whatever the user supplied, draft a message explaining the outcome without reciting the detection logic, and record every reinstatement as what it is: a labelled false positive. Reinstatement volume is where your real error rate hides, and few teams count it.

Where AI fails here

Irreversible actions stay with a human, and in software the irreversible action is larger than a decline. Terminating a tenant can destroy the only copy of a customer's data and revoke credentials their production depends on. No automated pipeline should hold that authority.

Anything that silently encodes a view about a country, a card type or a payment method is a liability even where the correlation is real. These proxies arrive quietly, through features that look technical, inherited from your own reviewers' past decisions.

The worst failure is a model learning from decisions that were themselves wrong. A ban nobody appealed is recorded as correct. A decline that lost a deal leaves no trace. Train on that and you industrialise last year's mistakes with more consistency than the people who made them.

Explaining too much fails as well: a detailed reason handed to an attacker is free feedback on your detection. And no model decides whether your free tier is too generous, or what your appetite for channel risk is. Those are commercial decisions, and automating around them moves the cost into the queue.

Pick the abuse you already pay for

Start with the loss you can already name. If your compute bill carries workloads that were never your product, instrument that. If your dispute process has quietly become a refund process, build the representment pack. If reviewers spend their first ten minutes assembling the same file from the same systems, build the file. The first deliverable is assembly and written reasoning, with actions limited to reversible rungs and reinstatements counted from the start.
