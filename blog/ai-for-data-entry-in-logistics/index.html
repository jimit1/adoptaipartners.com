AI for data entry in logistics: reading bookings, proof of delivery and EDI fallout without keying it again

29 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

The freight moves fine. It is the paperwork that moves slowly.

A load can be collected, trunked, cross docked and delivered inside twenty four hours, and the record of it can take a week to settle. Somebody types the booking into the transport management system from an email. Somebody else retypes part of it into the warehouse system so the goods can be received. A third keys the delivery outcome from a scanned consignment note. A fourth keys the carrier invoice against an accrual estimated by hand. None of those systems were joined, so the same shipment detail is entered three or four times by four people, each capable of a typo, and the typo surfaces weeks later as a rejected invoice.

This is the work that scales with volume. Trailers and forklifts do not need more headcount when volumes rise. The desk between the systems does, because the work between systems is done by people.

The same shipment, keyed four times

Draw the real path of one consignment through your own systems, not the path in the process document.

A customer sends a booking request. The transport office keys it into the TMS. Planning allocates it, which often means keying a variant of the same detail into a subcontractor portal or a spreadsheet the subbie actually reads. The warehouse needs the same consignee and reference to receive or dispatch, so it is keyed again. The driver returns a consignment note with a signature, a time, and often a handwritten line about a damaged pallet, which gets keyed as a delivery status and sometimes an exception. Then finance keys the carrier invoice, compares it to an accrual somebody estimated from a rate card, and chases the difference.

At every hop, the input is a document and the output is a typed field. That is the pattern worth attacking, whether you run forty vehicles or four hundred.

Where the keying actually happens

Booking intake

Bookings arrive in whatever form the customer prefers, because the customer is the customer. Free text in an email body. A PDF purchase order with the delivery address in a box that moved last quarter. A spreadsheet with twelve lines, three of which are actually two consignments. A portal export carrying the right data in the wrong column order. An operator reads it, decides what it means, and types the fields one at a time.

The judgement inside that is real. Deciding that "same as last week but 2 pallets not 1" refers to a standing collection, or that a window written as "am" means before noon for this account and before ten for another, is knowledge sitting in the operator's head. Any system that treats this as simple transcription will fail on the interesting half of the traffic.

Rates, surcharges and accruals

Rate entry is where money leaks. Fuel surcharges, waiting time, congestion and tolls, pallet exchange, out of hours delivery, failed collection charges. Somebody applies a rate card by reading it. The accrual posted at dispatch is frequently an educated guess, and the carrier invoice that arrives later is reconciled by eye, line by line, against a manifest.

Proof of delivery and consignment notes

POD capture is document reading dressed up as data entry. Scanned or photographed notes, sometimes at an angle, sometimes with a thumb over the corner. Handwritten driver annotations that carry the whole commercial story: two cartons short, pallet wrapped damaged, refused by goods in, gate closed, signed by initials only. Photographs of damage attached to a cab app with no structured field at all. Somebody looks at each one and decides whether this is a clean delivery, a shortage, a damage claim, or a redelivery.

Customer references and purchase order numbers

These must match exactly or the invoice is rejected. Not approximately, exactly, including the leading zeros and the prefix the customer's ERP insists on. A reference keyed with a letter O instead of a zero will pass every internal check, travel the whole way through the job, and fail at the customer's accounts payable weeks later, by which time the person who keyed it has forgotten the shipment.

Master data

Addresses, consignees, sites, gate instructions, opening hours. This is where duplicates breed. An operator under time pressure cannot find "Jones Retail DC 3" in the search box, so they create "Jones Retail Distribution Centre 3", and now reporting, routing and rating all split across two records for the same place.

EDI fallout

You already have EDI, and that is why fallout matters. Messages that arrive but fail validation land in a queue a person works through by hand: an unrecognised location code, a unit of measure the partner changed without telling anyone, a segment that arrived empty. Volume through EDI is high, so even a small failure rate produces a steady manual queue, and that queue is usually worked by whoever is least busy rather than whoever knows most.

What an AI system should actually do here

The useful framing is not "automate data entry". It is: read whatever arrived, propose a structured record, show your reasoning per field, and post only what you are confident about. Concretely, in the order we would usually build it.

Read bookings from any form they arrive in and write structured records into the TMS with a reason attached to every field, so an operator can see that the delivery date came from line 4 of the email body and the pallet count came from the attached PDF rather than from an assumption.

Match consignee and address against existing master data instead of creating records. Resolve "Jones Retail DC 3" to the record that already exists, and when the match is not clean, say so rather than inventing a second version of the site.

Read POD images and driver annotations into structured exception flags: short, damaged, refused, partial, pallets exchanged, with the crop of the handwriting attached so a human can check the reading at a glance rather than reopening the scan.

Clear EDI fallout by inferring the correct mapping from the partner's history and presenting it for confirmation. A person confirms, the system records who confirmed it, and the next identical case is easier.

Validate references and purchase order numbers against the customer's order before anything posts, so a mismatch is caught at intake rather than at invoice rejection.

Hold anything below a confidence threshold for a named owner. Not a shared queue, a named person, with the reason it stopped shown at the top.

And log everything, because automation without an audit trail is automation your auditors and your largest customer will eventually make you switch off.

Where the system must not decide alone

The boundary is not about difficulty. It is about reversibility.

Creating new master data stays with a human. A new consignee or site record is a lasting change to everyone's reporting and rating, and a duplicate created in error costs more to clean up than it saved.

Resolving a damage or shortage claim stays with a human. The system can flag that the driver wrote "2 short" and attach the photograph. Deciding what the organisation concedes is a commercial decision with money and a customer relationship attached.

Overriding a customer reference stays with a human. If the reference does not match the order, the correct behaviour is to stop and ask, never to helpfully correct it into something that looks right.

Anything with an irreversible financial consequence stays with a human. Releasing a carrier invoice for payment, adjusting an accrual, applying a surcharge that was not on the rate card, issuing a credit.

The pattern that works is narrow: the system reads, matches and drafts, at volume, with evidence. The person decides, on the small set of cases that genuinely need a decision.

Where AI fails here

It misreads handwriting, and confident misreading is worse than no reading. A driver's "2" that is really a "7" will post cleanly and quietly. Handwritten quantities need a confidence threshold set deliberately high or a mandatory second look, and expect to tune that per depot, because handwriting habits are local.

It overreaches on matching. A model tuned to reduce duplicates will eventually match a delivery to the wrong site in a group that has four addresses in one town. That error is expensive and hard to spot, because everything downstream looks plausible.

It cannot fix missing information. If the booking email genuinely does not state a delivery window, no system can extract one. The honest output is a gap flagged for a person, and any system that fills the gap with a sensible default is generating problems rather than reading documents.

It degrades when documents change. A customer redesigns their PDF, a partner changes a code list, a portal export adds a column. Extraction quality drops before anybody notices, which is why you monitor field level accuracy in production rather than trusting a launch test alone.

It struggles with implicit customer rules. The unwritten conventions experienced operators apply, this account always wants a tail lift, that site refuses anything after three, live in people's heads and not in any document the system can read. They have to be written down before they can be automated, and writing them down is most of the work.

And it will be judged against the wrong baseline. Manual keying is not error free, but its errors are familiar. A system that is more accurate overall can still feel worse when its mistakes are unfamiliar, so measure both against the same sample before launch.

How to know whether it worked

Measure before you build anything. Take a representative fortnight of bookings, PODs and EDI fallout, and record the honest numbers: how many documents, how many fields each, how long per document, how many touched twice, how many invoices rejected for reference mismatches, how many duplicate master data records created. Then set the gate: accuracy per field type, share of traffic straight through, share held for review, and who owns each held case.

After launch, what matters is the share of consignments that post without a human touch, the accuracy on the cases that did post, the time to clear the held queue, and whether invoice rejections for data reasons actually fell. Where to start

Start with your highest volume single document type, usually booking intake from your largest accounts, or POD capture if invoicing is where the pain sits. One document type, one named owner for the held queue, one measured baseline. Prove the reading is trustworthy there, then widen.
