AI for Invoice Processing in Construction: Applications, Valuations and Retention

14 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

If you are a main contractor or developer paying subcontractors, suppliers and consultants, the word invoice is doing very little work. Most of what you pay against is an application for payment: a claim for the value of work done on a project to date, measured against a subcontract order, adjusted for variations, subject to retention, and due under the payment cycle that contract sets.

Say this early. Payment notice regimes, withholding notices, retention practice and tax deduction schemes for construction payments differ by country, and by which standard form your contract uses. What follows is the shape of the problem, not legal advice.

Why a three way match does not describe this world

A three way match asks whether the quantity billed against a purchase order arrived. In construction there is usually no receipt event, because nothing was delivered. Work was performed, and its value is a matter of measurement and opinion. A subcontract order exists, but it is a framework, not a line by line shopping list. The real reference is the valuation, itself a document your quantity surveyor produces.

The cumulative application, and why an error never stays in one period

Applications are cumulative. Each states the gross value of everything done since the job started, deducts what has been certified, and claims the balance. Every application depends on every one before it, so an error does not sit still. A quantity over-valued early carries forward and becomes the accepted baseline. Correcting it later looks like a deduction from money already agreed, the conversation nobody wants on a live site.

This suits an agent, because it is arithmetic with memory. It holds the certified position for every package and period, and reports where this month's gross figure is inconsistent with the last agreed one rather than simply larger.

Reconciling this month's application

Against the subcontract order and the variation account

The agent lines each claimed item up against the subcontract order, checks the rate claimed against the rate in the order, and separates measured work from variation work so the two are not blended. Items in neither the order nor the agreed variation account are reported as unsupported, with the value stated.

Against the programme and what site recorded

Value claimed should bear some relationship to progress achieved. The agent compares claimed completion against the programme, site progress records, inspection sign offs and any measurement the project team has captured, then flags packages where claimed progress runs ahead of anything recorded. It does not decide the true figure. It tells your surveyor where to look.

Notice deadlines are the sharpest risk here

Treat this as the highest value automation on a construction payables desk, well above extraction.

Under many contract forms and jurisdictions, payment is governed by notices with hard deadlines. A notice stating the sum you consider due must be issued within the period the contract sets. If you intend to pay less, a withholding notice must follow within its own period and say what the contract requires. Miss those windows and in several regimes the sum applied for becomes the sum payable, whether or not the work was done.

The agent reads the payment framework out of each contract, builds the dates for every package and cycle, tracks what has been received and issued, and escalates with time on the clock. It drafts. It never issues. A notice has legal effect and belongs to an authorised person.

Variations, instructions and the rate nobody agreed

Variations reach applications long before they reach agreement. Work is instructed verbally on site, done immediately because the programme demands it, priced later, then claimed at a rate nobody accepted.

The agent traces rather than prices. For each claimed variation it looks for the instruction that authorised it: the written instruction, the confirmation of a verbal one, the change record, the drawing revision, the meeting minute. It then reports one of three states. There is a valid instruction and the claim can be assessed. There is an instruction, but the rate differs from the basis the contract sets for valuing change. Or there is no instruction at all, which is the finding that saves most money.

Dayworks, labour and plant hours

Dayworks sheets arrive signed, unsigned, or signed by someone with no authority. The agent reconciles claimed labour and plant hours against site attendance, allocation sheets and plant hire records, then reports sheets with no authorised signature and hours with no corresponding record. Whether to pay an unsigned sheet is a commercial decision. Knowing it is unsigned should not require reading the whole pack.

Retention, and the money nobody chases

Retention is deducted from each payment at a typical retention percentage, released in part at practical completion and in part at the end of the defects liability period. On money you hold this is usually managed. On money owed to you it often is not, because chasing it is nobody's job once the site team has moved on.

An agent maintains both ledgers: deducted per package per period, balance held, the release triggers each contract sets and the dates they were met. Then it raises the claim when it falls due. That is cash already earned and forgotten.

Materials on site and off site

Applications routinely include materials delivered but not installed, and sometimes materials still in a supplier's yard. Whether either is payable depends on the contract, and off site materials usually carry conditions: vesting, evidence of ownership, insurance, identification and storage. The agent checks the claim against what the contract allows, reports missing evidence, and watches for the double count where materials paid for once reappear in the value of installed work.

Compliance checked at the payment date, not at onboarding

Most organisations verify insurances, qualifications, competence records and registration under any tax deduction scheme for construction payments that applies in your market at onboarding, then never again. Certificates expire mid project. An agent checks them at every payment date: cover in date, professional indemnity where the package carries design, the competence records it requires, and the deduction status setting what you must withhold. Missing evidence surfaces before the payment run, not in an audit.

Cost coding and the cost value reconciliation

Cost must land on the right project, phase, package and cost head, because the commercial team runs the job from the cost value reconciliation. Miscoding does not merely put a number on the wrong ledger line. It makes one package look profitable and another look like it is bleeding, and the resourcing, forecasting and final account decisions taken off that report are then wrong. The agent codes from the subcontract order, the project structure and the precedent of previous payments on the package, and routes anything novel to a person.

Applied, certified and paid

Three numbers must be traceable for every period: what was applied for, what you certified, and what you paid after retention, deductions, contra charges and any statutory withholding. They are rarely the same, and the differences are the substance of most payment disputes. An agent holds that three column position per package per period, with the reason for each difference attached to the line that caused it. Where you self bill, it verifies your own payment document against the valuation before it goes out.

Multiple projects, joint ventures and shared suppliers

The same supplier invoices several of your entities at once: a project company, a joint venture, a special purpose vehicle, the parent. References collide and queries arrive without saying which entity they concern. An agent resolves each document to the right project and legal entity first, reconciles statements across all of them, and flags anything paid by one entity then presented to another.

Where judgement stays human

Two decisions must never drift to an agent. Certifying a valuation is a judgement about the value of work performed, with contractual and legal consequence, and it stays with your quantity surveyor. Payment timing is a commercial decision, because a contractor is often paying subcontractors before the client has paid for the same work. Which payments go in which run, and which relationships you protect when cash is tight, carry consequences an agent cannot weigh.

The agent prepares the pack, carries the evidence and calculates the dates, so your surveyor is never the reason a deadline was missed.

How to evaluate this before you trust it

Do not accept a demonstration on a vendor's sample documents. Run it in parallel on a closed out project where you know the answers, and compare its reconciliation against what your team concluded at the time. The disagreements matter, because some will be the agent being wrong and some will be things the original valuation missed.

Measure separately rather than as one accuracy figure. Extraction on typed applications and on handwritten dayworks sheets are different numbers. Deadline tracking should be measured for completeness, because a missed deadline is a different class of failure from a mis-read total.

Insist on shadow running before authority widens, and agree in writing what the agent may write back and what it may only draft.

Where AI fails here

Measurement is not extraction. Judging whether a claimed completion figure on a partially built element is right requires knowing the works. An agent can find the inconsistency between claim and record. It cannot value the work.

Site records that do not exist cannot be reconciled against. If progress is not recorded, or dayworks sit on paper that never reaches the commercial team, the agent can say a claim is unsupported but not whether it is wrong. Handwritten and photographed documents also extract less reliably than clean digital ones, and construction produces plenty of both.

Informally varied terms defeat the agent completely. If the parties agreed a different payment cycle in a meeting and never wrote it down, an agent reading the executed contract will calculate the wrong dates with total confidence. Unwritten internal policy does the same. If your rules on paying unsigned dayworks or setting contra charges live in experienced surveyors' heads, writing them down comes first.

Confident errors happen. A model can produce a reconciliation that reads perfectly and is wrong, which is why validation against your commercial system, confidence thresholds and human sign off on anything that moves money or affects a notice are structural requirements, not settings.
