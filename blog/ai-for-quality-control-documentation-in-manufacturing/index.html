AI for Quality Control Documentation in Manufacturing: Approval Packs, Nonconformances and the Customer Response

25 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A machined bracket can be set up, cut, deburred and measured in an afternoon. The pack proving it was made correctly takes a fortnight, and it is written by the two or three engineers who are also the only people on site able to get a struggling line running again.

Most of this documentation is assembly rather than authorship. The drawing exists, the measurements exist, the certificate arrived with the bar, the risk analysis was reviewed at launch. Somebody has to gather those things, check that they agree, arrange them the way the customer's quality engineer expects, and notice what is missing before the customer does.

So the question is which parts of that assembly an agent can carry to the point of review, and which must stay with a named engineer.

Why the part is quick and the pack is slow

The evidence for one part is scattered across systems never designed to be read together. Calibration records live in one person's spreadsheet. The risk analysis has not been opened since the launch team disbanded. The customer's actual requirement is buried in a purchase order field or a note inside the customer's supplier portal. Assembling the pack means walking between all of them with a mental checklist, and the failure mode is interruption rather than carelessness.

The production part approval pack as an assembly job

A production part approval submission, or a first article report, is a dependency graph pretending to be a folder: process flow, risk analysis, control plan, results covering every characteristic, material testing, gauge studies, capability evidence, warrant on top. An agent can read the characteristic list off the drawing, match each characteristic to a result, and list the ones with nothing against them. It checks the revision quoted on every document against the one being submitted, and applies the customer specific requirements, including the dull ones about form version.

What the agent must not fill in

The warrant signature. The judgement that capability evidence is sufficient. And never a plausible value for a characteristic that was not measured. The missing list is the part everyone wants to skip and the only part that makes the rest trustworthy.

Control plans that drift from the risk analysis

After an engineering change the control plan gets updated, because production is waiting on it. The risk analysis often does not, because nobody is waiting on it. The drift stays invisible until an auditor reads them side by side and asks why a failure mode with the highest severity ranking carries no detection control. An agent can hold that link continuously, raising the mismatches: a control with no corresponding failure mode, a reaction plan naming an instruction that no longer exists. Keeping two documents in step is a task with no owner and no deadline.

Inspection records and the revision they were taken against

Measurement reports are often accurate and quietly useless, because nothing durable records which drawing revision they were taken against. Months later, when a customer disputes a dimension, you can produce a number but not prove what it was compared to. An agent writing the record at capture attaches the revision, instrument and operator automatically, and compares the nominal on the report against the current drawing. A mismatch there is the earliest available signal that somebody is inspecting to superseded paper.

Calibration evidence and the reading nobody questioned

The expensive calibration event is not a schedule slip. It is a reading taken on an instrument already past its due date. The question that follows is what was measured with it since the last good calibration, which parts those measurements released, and which have shipped. An agent can watch due dates against usage records and assemble that exposure without anyone reconstructing it: every record naming the instrument in the window, the parts involved, the shipments that carried them. Whether the readings stand, and whether the customer is told, is not its call.

Certificates out, certificates in

Outbound certificates of conformity are usually retyped from other documents, which is where transcription errors enter a signed claim. An agent can assemble one from the source records and flag a conformity claim with no evidence behind it. Inbound certificates are the larger opportunity. Checking one against the specification the order demanded is comparing values to ranges and names to lists, work goods receipt rarely has time for, so certificates are filed unread. An agent raises the property out of range, the required property absent, or the heat identity missing from the delivery paperwork.

The supplier who sends a photograph

Some suppliers send a photograph of a certificate taken on a bench, half in shadow. An agent reads it into structured fields and records which fields it read with low confidence, so a human checks the doubtful values rather than checking everything or nothing.

Nonconformance records, rework and concessions

The raw material of a nonconformance record is a line of handwriting: holes not lining up at second operation, told the supervisor. Everything else is reconstructed later by somebody who was not there. An agent can turn that note into a structured draft covering part and operation, quantity affected and containment. Then it does the hardest part, keeping the record moving: drafting the rework instruction, writing the concession request with the deviation bounded by quantity or date, and chasing stalled records by naming the person and the missing item. Disposition and root cause stay human.

The customer complaint response and its two clocks

A complaint runs on two clocks: a short one for containment, often in hours, and a long one for the root cause behind it. Teams miss the short clock by trying to answer both at once. An agent can draft the containment response from shipment and inventory data rather than memory: which stock is affected, what has been sorted, what is on hold, and what the customer should check in their own stock. On the long clock it drafts each section once it becomes answerable, keeps internal and customer facing versions consistent, and follows up committed actions at the agreed intervals.

Layered process audits that quietly stop happening

Layered audits are scheduled, done diligently for a while, and then a launch arrives and they fall off the week. An agent can schedule them, prompt the right person, and draft the record from short answers so completing one takes minutes. More useful is what it reads across them: the question that keeps failing on the same cell, the finding raised repeatedly with a different corrective action each time. A question that fails again and again is a process problem wearing an audit's clothes.

The sample the auditor picks

An external auditor picks a sample and follows it end to end, and you cannot know which one. An agent can run that walk on a random selection at intervals and report where the chain breaks before an auditor does.

Which shipments contain the affected lot

A supplier notifies a problem against a material identity, or an operator finds a fault that may have been present for weeks, and a customer is on the telephone asking which shipments are affected. Behind that sits a chain from goods receipt to material lot to work order to serial identity to dispatch note to customer, crossing purchasing, production and quality systems and at least one spreadsheet. An agent with read access resolves the chain and names every link it could not establish rather than smoothing over it, because an honest gap tells you what to fix.

Engineering change records and what the part should have been

Change records sit underneath everything above, because they decide what the part should have been in the first place. The cost of a change spreads across the drawing, the control plan, the risk analysis, the work instructions, the gauges and the cutover point in production. From an approved change an agent can produce the impact list: every document naming the affected characteristic or operation, every open work order crossing the effective date. It tracks what has moved and flags the mismatch that causes real damage, stock made after the effective date under the previous instruction. Whether the change requires customer notification stays with a person.

Where AI fails here

Root cause attribution is where fluent writing is most dangerous. An agent can produce a well organised narrative reading as though the cause is settled when the evidence supports three explanations equally. Treat every drafted cause as a hypothesis with its evidence attached.

Disposition and release are not delegable. Use as is, scrap, sort, concession, first shipment approval: each carries liability and needs a qualified person's name against it.

Drawing interpretation is harder than it looks. An agent can read the characters on a drawing. Reading a geometric tolerance correctly, with its datum scheme and material condition modifiers, is a different skill, and a wrong reading propagates silently into an inspection plan. Measurement context is invisible too: a value inside tolerance does not prove a good part if the fixture was wrong.

Data quality sets the ceiling on everything else. Part numbers that changed during a system migration, lots split without a record, and poor scans produce confidently wrong bounding of affected material, which warns the wrong customers and leaves the right ones in the dark.

Access is a practical limit, since much of this documentation lives inside customer portals with their own credentials. And agents are strongest where history holds something similar, weakest on genuinely new failures, so escalate novelty loudly rather than filing it under the nearest category.

Choosing the first part family to pilot on

Pick one part family rather than one document type, ideally one with a documentation history you can check the agent against, because the point of a first deployment is comparison rather than coverage.

Inbound certificate checking and approval pack assembly are the strongest starting places. Both are verifiable against documents you already hold, both produce a draft rather than a decision, and neither touches a disposition.

Before anything is built, write down what the agent may read, what it may write back into your quality system, and what always routes to a named person. Then run it alongside the existing process for a full cycle and compare in both directions: not only what the agent missed, but what it caught that the engineer did not.

If the people who could solve your quality problems spend their weeks writing the documents that describe them, the question is which of those documents an agent could draft to the point of review, and which must never leave a human hand.
