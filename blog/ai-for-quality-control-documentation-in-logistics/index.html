AI for Quality Control Documentation in Logistics

18 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A pallet of chilled product is refused at a retailer's delivery point and comes back to the warehouse. Whether it can still be sold rests on a logger download nobody has opened, a goods received note signed by a colleague on nights, two photographs taken on a handheld in poor dock light, and the client's specification for that product, which is not the one for the near identical carton beside it.

None of that evidence was created to settle a dispute. All of it is about to be read as though it was.

Quality documentation here is not a production record. Nothing is made, so there is no specification to conform to. What you hold is condition and custody evidence about goods belonging to somebody else, in motion while you had them, judged against a standard the client set. It answers three questions: who damaged this, was the chain unbroken, and can we prove it to the cargo owner, an auditor or the recall.

That is why completeness beats depth: a thorough inspection with no timestamp is weaker evidence than a thin one carrying a time, a place, a name and a photograph.

A different specification for every client on the same dock

In a third party warehouse the same carton arrives for three clients with three tolerances: one accepts a crushed corner if the inner pack is intact, one rejects any outer damage because the carton is the retail unit, one wants a photograph of every pallet regardless.

Nobody holds that across a shift, so operators fall back on a house standard, and the house standard is nobody's contract. An agent can carry each client's receiving specification as live rules and put the right question to the operator while the pallet is in front of them: a date code here, a photograph there, a defect that is a rejection rather than a note.

The receipt check that decides who pays months later

Damage and discrepancy at receipt is the highest value record in the building, because it is the only moment at which inbound damage can still be pinned on the carrier or supplier rather than on you.

In practice it is a note, a count and sometimes a photograph, made with vehicles queuing outside. From that an agent can build a structured condition record: received against expected, the shortage or overage, the damage in words that mean something, the carrier, the seal, the time. It can also list the discrepancies that never became a claim.

What the photograph shows and what the note claims

Dock photographs are the most common and least useful evidence in logistics. There are thousands, taken in bad light, filed where nobody looks until something goes wrong.

A vision capable agent can read the image and the free text together and say whether they agree. Is there a readable pallet label matching the receipt. Does the visible damage match a note that says water damage. Is there a photograph at all against a receipt flagged as damaged, since the usual failure is a missing one rather than a wrong one.

Logger downloads, and an excursion that has a duration

A logger download is a long series of readings, and the question is never what the temperature was. It is whether there was an excursion, when it started, how long it lasted, and whether the product sat outside its limit long enough to matter.

Reconciling a download against the consignment is clerical, and an agent does it well: tie the logger serial to the load it travelled with, find every departure from the client's range, state its start time and duration, and separate artefacts from real events, since a spike while a trailer door stands open is not a sustained overnight rise. The output is an excursion summary with the trace behind it, and the disposition is somebody else's.

Lot codes taken at the door, or never

Everything that makes a recall survivable happens at receipt: batch or lot, best before date, which pallet went to which location, and which customer order later drew from it. Captured properly, a recall is a query. Captured loosely, it is a physical search with the client on the phone.

An agent can check lot capture where it happens rather than where it is needed. Lines received with no lot. Codes that do not match the format that client always uses. One lot spelled two ways.

The keyed date that breaks rotation without an error

Rotation fails silently. First expired first out works perfectly on wrong data: one date keyed with a transposed month and the system picks that pallet first, or holds it at the back for a year. An agent comparing a keyed date against the carton photograph, the shelf life the specification allows and the other dates on the receipt catches the implausible ones that day.

Declarations, segregation and labelling on restricted freight

Dangerous goods paperwork has the least tolerance for improvisation: a packing declaration, the right marks and labels, segregation that respects what may not load next to what, and a record showing the check happened before the vehicle left.

An agent can test a declaration for internal consistency against what the documents state, flag a mismatch between it and the labelling evidence on file, and confirm a segregation check exists for a load that needed one. What it must never do is reach for the classification.

Seals, and the two numbers that should match

Container and trailer security evidence is one small comparison done consistently: the seal number at loading, the seal number at opening, the condition of the seal, plus the inspection record.

It fails constantly: the number is handwritten in one place, keyed in another, and nobody reads both. An agent can reconcile seal numbers across the loading record, the transport document and the receiving check, then raise mismatches and blanks while the unit is still on site. A discrepancy found at the gate is manageable; found in an investigation, it is an admission.

Returns grading, where two operators disagree about one item

In returns the same item is called saleable by one grader and scrap by another. The standard exists, usually with photographs, and is still applied differently across shifts because its words are softer than the decisions taken from them.

An agent cannot grade the item. It can compare the photographs and notes attached to graded returns against the client's standard, surface disposals inconsistent with similar items passed as saleable, and show a supervisor where two people read one rule two ways.

Records that belong to the building, not a consignment

Food and pharmaceutical storage carries records tied to no individual consignment: cleaning schedules signed by shift, pest control visit reports and their recommendations, dock door checks, temperature mapping of the chamber itself.

An agent holding the required frequencies can report what was actually recorded. Periods with no entry. Visit recommendations never closed out. Sign offs by someone the rota says was not working.

Three shifts, one procedure, several laminated copies

Every record above is only as good as the answer to a question asked late: which version of the procedure was in force that day, and had the operator who signed been trained on that one. Usually the controlled version sits in a document system, the copy in the wall sleeve is older, and nothing ties either to a shift.

An agent can maintain one answer: the version live on each date, the sign offs recorded against it, and the people who signed a superseded revision after a new one was issued.

When the customer audits you instead of the goods

The hardest documentation event in logistics is a client's quality team auditing your site. They arrive with a checklist built for their business and expect evidence organised the way they think rather than the way your systems store it. The records exist. They were never kept for this.

An agent reading those records continuously can produce the pack: every document named, dated, attributed and ordered against the audit scope, with the contradictions found before the auditor finds them. A gap you disclose, with a plan attached, is a different conversation from one uncovered in front of you.

Chasing the signature, then answering the finding

Most findings are missing signatures, missing dates and unclosed actions rather than bad practice, and an agent holding a completeness rule per record type can chase the missing element while the file is still open and the people are still on shift. The corrective action request that follows is usually answered with the fix described and the cause asserted rather than investigated. An agent can assemble the factual history behind it, draft the response in the client's structure, and track the commitment dates.

Where AI fails here

Releasing product to sell after a temperature excursion is a named quality person's decision and must stay one. An agent can show the excursion, its duration and the precedent. It cannot carry the consequence of being wrong, and a client who learns the release call was automated will doubt every earlier one.

A photograph is evidence, not a judgement. Vision models read a label well and judge poorly between a scuff that does not matter and a crush that compromised the inner pack. Treat image reading as retrieval and consistency checking, not as a condition call.

Dangerous goods classification cannot be inferred. Where the paperwork is ambiguous the answer is a trained person and the shipper, not a model's best guess.

An agent cannot certify what nobody recorded. If the night shift took no photographs there are none, and the only honest output is a gap report. A plausible record written to fill the hole is worse than the hole.

A corrective action is a commitment a company makes, not a document it generates. Fluent responses with nothing behind them fail the effectiveness check and cost more trust than a blunt admission.

Where to start

Pick one client and one record type. Goods-in damage and discrepancy is usually the best first choice: the evidence is created in one place, the standard is written in a contract you already hold, and a recovered claim shows up in the same quarter. Cold chain reconciliation is next best if you store chilled or frozen goods.

Run it in parallel for a month alongside your team, and compare the two on the cases that went wrong rather than the clean ones. The measure is whether the next audit pack or claim file assembles itself without a week of archaeology, and whether you knew about the gaps first.
