AI Use Cases in Technology Teams: The Work Around the Work

2 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Ask an engineering leader where the week goes and you will rarely hear "writing code". You hear about the incident review open for nine days because nobody has assembled the timeline. The access request sitting behind eleven others. The security questionnaire that landed on a staff engineer because they answered the last one. The release notes reconstructed from commit messages the morning after the deploy.

None of that is engineering. All of it has to happen, most of it has to be right, and it is almost entirely assembly work: gathering facts that already exist somewhere and putting them into the shape a person or a system expects.

That is the part worth handing to an agent. The pattern is the same every time: the agent reads the systems of record, assembles the draft, and stops. A named human decides and owns the outcome. What follows is where that split holds up in a technology organisation, and where it does not.

The load nobody staffs for

Most of this work has no owner. It is absorbed by whoever is nearest: the tech lead, the on-call engineer, the one platform person who knows how the identity provider is configured. It never appears on a roadmap, so it is never resourced, so it is always late and always done at the expense of something planned.

It also has a quality problem. Work done at the end of a long day, under mild resentment, produces the thin incident record, the over-broad permission grant, the questionnaire answer promising something the product does not do.

Incidents and what happens after them

Incident write ups

The facts of an incident are already recorded. They sit across the alerting system, the chat channel, the deployment log, the ticket and the customer messages. Nobody wants to spend two hours stitching them together, so the write up is done from memory a week later and loses the detail that made it useful.

An agent assembles the timeline instead: the first alert and the first human acknowledgement, the point at which impact was understood, the mitigations attempted in order, the change that resolved it. It records what customers saw and for how long, against your own severity definitions, and cites where each fact came from so a reader can check.

What it does not do is state a cause. Contributing factors, the honest account of why a control did not catch this, and the actions that follow are human judgements made with the people involved. The agent removes the transcription work that stops that conversation happening while memory is fresh.

On-call handover

Handover is usually a message written in a hurry that says "quiet shift, watch the queue depth". The next engineer inherits no context and rediscovers it at three in the morning.

An agent drafts the handover from the shift itself: alerts that fired and how each was resolved, anything silenced and when the silence expires, changes deployed in the window, tickets still open, work planned into the next shift. The outgoing engineer edits and adds the thing no system knows, usually a hunch about what is fragile right now.

The request queues

Ticket triage

Internal queues fail at the front, not the back. Requests arrive misclassified, missing the one field that would let anyone act, and land on the wrong team twice before reaching the right one.

A triage agent reads what arrives, in whatever form, and classifies it against your existing taxonomy. It identifies the affected system and environment, extracts the requester, the business impact and any error text, checks whether an open ticket already covers the same thing, and links related history. Where a field is missing it drafts the clarifying question rather than bouncing the ticket back with a template.

It proposes a priority against your written criteria and suggests an owner. A human confirms both, because priority is a resourcing decision, particularly when the requester is senior enough to have opinions about it.

Access requests, joiners, movers and leavers

Access administration is high volume, repetitive and consequential. It is also where over-provisioning happens, because approving is quick and questioning is slow.

An agent resolves who is asking, their role, their manager and their existing entitlements, then compares the ask against the role definition. It states plainly whether this is standard, an expansion beyond the role, or a combination that breaks a segregation of duties rule you have defined, and drafts the approval package with the evidence attached. For movers it produces the difference between old role and new, including what should be removed, the step routinely skipped. For leavers it assembles the inventory across systems, including the accounts outside your identity provider that are always forgotten.

The agent grants nothing. Provisioning runs through your existing tooling after a named approver decides. What the agent fixes is the real bottleneck, which is making that decision quickly and well informed.

Internal support and the how do I queue

Much internal engineering support is answering a question that is already documented, or was answered in a channel four months ago. An agent that retrieves and quotes your own runbooks, architecture notes and past threads, pointing at where the answer lives, absorbs a real share of that traffic. When it cannot find an answer it says so and routes the question, and the gap becomes a documentation task rather than a recurring interruption.

Shipping and the paperwork it generates

Release documentation and change records

Release notes, change tickets and deployment records exist for auditors, support teams and customers, and get written by engineers who have already moved on.

An agent drafts from what actually shipped: merged changes grouped by component, the tickets they close, the customer visible behaviour that differs, configuration or migration steps required, and the rollback path. It produces the change record in the format your process demands, with the risk fields pre-populated, and separately drafts the customer facing note in plain language. An engineer reviews. The value is that a draft exists at deploy time rather than a week later.

Runbook and documentation drift

Documentation goes stale silently. An agent can compare runbooks against current reality, flagging services that no longer exist, renamed dashboards, escalation contacts who left and steps referencing a deprecated tool. It drafts the correction for review, and never edits an operational runbook unattended.

Vendor, licence and cloud administration

Every technology organisation of size loses money and attention here. Renewals arrive without notice. Licences stay assigned to people who left. Cloud spend moves and nobody notices until the quarter closes.

An agent maintains the picture. It reconciles licence assignments against active staff and actual usage and drafts a reclaim list with names and last activity. It tracks renewal dates against contract terms and prepares the pre-renewal brief a month out: what we pay, what we use, what changed, what the notice period is. It drafts the spend variance note showing which service moved and which change preceded it. Procurement and finance decide, without the week of gathering that normally precedes the decision.

Security questionnaires and audit evidence

Security questionnaires block deals and consume senior engineering time, and the answers are largely repeated from previous questionnaires and control descriptions you already maintain.

An agent matches each incoming question against your approved answer library, drafts a response quoting the source, and marks its confidence. Anything without a strong match is flagged as genuinely new and goes to a person. The rule that matters: an agent never invents a control that does not exist and never softens a "no" into a "partially". A questionnaire answer is a contractual representation.

Audit evidence follows the same split. Collecting the sample, the ticket, the approval record and the log extract is assembly. Attesting that a control operated is not.

A brief and honest word on coding assistants

Coding assistants are real and useful, particularly for tests, boilerplate, unfamiliar syntax and explaining code nobody remembers writing. They are also the least differentiating AI investment available to you, since your competitors have the same tools, and they do not touch the load described above. In one sense they add to it: more code merged means more change records, more review, more documentation and more incidents to write up. If your constraint is administrative rather than typing speed, start there.

What makes a good first candidate

Pick a queue where the inputs are already digital, the rules are written down somewhere you can point to, and a human review step already exists that you intend to keep. Incident write ups, release notes and access request preparation usually qualify. Anything where the agent would be the last check before a production or security consequence does not.

Run it alongside the current process for a few weeks and compare outputs. Where the agent disagrees with your team, fix the escalation rules rather than the prompt.

Where AI fails here

Models fabricate confidently. An agent asked why a service failed will produce a fluent explanation that fits the evidence and is wrong. This is why incident causation stays with people and why every assembled fact carries a citation to its source.

Silent omission is worse than error. A timeline accurate about the five things it found and silent about the sixth reads as complete, and reviewers trust complete looking documents. Build sampling into the process and keep it after go live.

Access and change decisions carry real blast radius. An agent permitted to grant entitlements or execute changes is a new privileged identity in your estate, and it will be a target. Keep agents on read and draft, and let your existing provisioning and deployment systems execute after a human decision, with the same logging you demand of staff.

Context windows are not memory. An agent handed a long incident channel weights what it saw most recently and loses the middle. Messy sources need structuring, not trust.

Some of this work is political, not clerical. A priority call, a renewal negotiation, a questionnaire answer a customer will hold you to: those are positions your organisation is taking. The draft can be assembled. The position cannot be delegated.
