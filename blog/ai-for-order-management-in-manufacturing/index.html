AI for order management in manufacturing: order entry, change orders and promised dates

24 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

An order desk in a discrete manufacturer does not process orders. It reconciles them. A purchase order arrives as a PDF attachment from a buyer who typed it out of their own system, quoting their part number, their unit of measure, a price from a framework agreement signed three years ago, and a date somebody in their plant asked for without looking at your lead time. Your job is to turn that into an ERP line production, finance and logistics can all live with.

Everyone outside the function thinks this is data entry. Data entry is the ten minutes. The rest is translation, checking and arguing, and then the change order that arrives the following week and undoes half of it.

The order arrives in four shapes

Very few manufacturers have one intake channel. EDI orders from the large accounts land structured and mostly clean, which is why people assume the problem is solved. Portal orders arrive on the customer's terms: somebody logs in, downloads the order and keys it in, and the portal is also where acknowledgements and date confirmations have to go back. Then the long tail: PDF attachments, spreadsheets, scanned order forms, and plain email saying "usual order, plus 40 of the bracket we did in June".

An intake agent reads the mailbox and the portals, recognises which messages are orders at all, and extracts header and lines into a structured draft. What matters more than the extraction is that all four channels become one queue with one status, so the desk stops working from three inboxes and a browser tab.

Part number translation

The customer orders their part number. If a distributor sits in between, the order may carry the distributor's number instead. Neither is your SKU. Somebody, usually one experienced person, knows that the customer's 4471-A is your 22-RB-4471 in the black finish, and that the same customer orders it under two numbers because they merged with a company that had its own numbering.

An agent can hold that mapping properly. It matches on the cross reference table you already have, and where there is no entry it proposes a match from the description text, the drawing revision, the price paid historically and what that customer has bought before, with the evidence attached.

The design choice that matters is what happens on a weak match. It should not guess into the ERP. It holds the line, proposes, and a person confirms once, after which the mapping is stored and never asked again. Most desks have thousands of these translations living in one or two heads.

Superseded and revision controlled parts

The harder case is a superseded part, ordered in good faith under the old number. Shipping the replacement without a conversation causes an incoming inspection failure at the customer's end, so the agent should flag it rather than resolve it silently.

Configured and made to order lines

On configured items the specification sits in the order text, not the part number. A line reads as a base model plus a string of options, or as a length, a material, a coating and a connection type in the customer's own shorthand.

An agent can parse that text against your configurator's option set, map each phrase to a characteristic, and produce a proposed configuration with the unresolved fields listed. It catches forbidden combinations before confirmation rather than after the order reaches engineering. What it cannot do is invent a missing characteristic. If the customer did not state the seal material, the useful output is that question drafted with everything else already resolved.

Quantity and unit of measure

This is where the money is lost. The customer orders 10. You sell in boxes of 25. They meant 10 boxes, or 10 pieces, and the order says "10 EA" because their system has one unit. Cable ordered in metres against a stock unit of coils. Fasteners ordered in thousands against a price per hundred.

An agent should check every line's quantity and unit against the customer's order history for that part, your selling unit, pack size, minimum order quantity, and the order value implied. A line computing to forty times that customer's normal spend on the part is a unit of measure error until proven otherwise. Catching it before confirmation is worth more than the intake automation, because it happens on the day rather than at goods receipt.

Price, discount and the framework agreement

Buyers quote the price they believe applies. It may come from a contract, a framework agreement, a volume tier, an annual price list that changed in January, or a quotation that expired.

An agent checks the ordered price against the contract price, the current list, any volume break the order triggers and any surcharge that applies, then produces a variance per line with the source of the price it expected. Within a tolerance you set it passes. Outside it, the line goes to the commercial owner with the comparison done. Accepting an under price is sometimes right. The agent's job is to make sure it is a decision and not an accident.

Incoterms and delivery terms

The delivery term on the order is often whatever the buyer's template says, and it contradicts your commercial agreement. That stays invisible until the freight invoice arrives or a customs document is wrong.

An agent compares the stated incoterm and named place against the agreed term for that customer and shipping location, checks the delivery address is one you hold, and flags where the term implies export documentation or a packing specification the order does not mention. The term is contractual, and changing it is not an order desk edit.

Requested date against what you can promise

Every order carries a requested date and most are aspirational. The confirmable date comes from available to promise, the lead time on a make item, component availability, and sometimes a capacity check the ERP alone will not do honestly.

An agent can run that check the moment the order is drafted, split it into lines that confirm as requested and lines that do not, and draft the customer facing explanation for the second group with the proposed date and the reason. Planning still owns the promise. What changes is that the desk sees the conflict at order entry rather than at the acknowledgement deadline, while there is time to act.

Acknowledgements and change orders

Order acknowledgements are a deadline, not a courtesy. Many customers require one within a fixed window, on their portal, in their format, and score you on it.

Change orders are the larger workload. A quantity moves, a date moves, a line is cancelled, an address changes, and each change ripples into the confirmed date, the allocation, the shipment and sometimes the price. Many desks spend more hours on changes than on original orders, and the changes arrive as prose in an email thread.

An agent reads the change request, identifies which order and lines it touches, works out what actually changed against the current confirmed position rather than the original order, and produces the delta. It re-runs the availability check on the affected lines only, drafts the revised acknowledgement, and files the change with an audit trail. Where a change falls inside your frozen period or triggers a cancellation charge, it says so before anyone agrees to it.

Schedule agreements and call offs

Automotive style supply does not work in discrete orders. There is a schedule agreement, then releases: firm quantities, planned quantities, cumulative received against cumulative shipped, all revised weekly.

The work is reconciliation. An agent compares each release against the previous one, identifies changes inside the firm horizon that should not have moved, checks cumulative figures against your own shipped quantity, and surfaces drift before it becomes an argument about who owes whom what. It also watches for releases that stop arriving, often the first sign of a problem nobody has mentioned yet.

Expedite requests

Expedites arrive as urgency without information. An agent can turn each one into a decision packet: what is available, what would have to move to satisfy it, what that displaces for another customer, and whether the account has an expedite clause. Planning and commercial then decide. The agent removes the twenty minutes of digging that currently happens under pressure, and keeps a record of who asks and how often.

Where AI fails here

Extraction from bad documents is still bad. A scanned order with a handwritten amendment in the margin, a table spanning a page break, or a fax quality PDF will produce confident and wrong line data. The failure is not that it errs, it is that it errs fluently, and a well formatted draft invites nobody to open the original. Keep the source document next to every extracted line.

Ambiguous specification cannot be resolved by inference. When the order text under specifies a configured item, the honest answer is a question to the customer. An agent filling the gap from the most common historical configuration will be right often enough to be trusted and wrong expensively.

Promised dates are not the agent's to give. Availability logic is only as good as the master data behind it, and every manufacturer has lead times in the item master nobody has reviewed since the last ERP upgrade. An agent confirming dates from stale data will confirm them beautifully and miss them all.

Commercial exceptions stay with people. Accepting a price variance, waiving a minimum order quantity, agreeing a change inside the frozen window, allocating scarce stock between two customers who both need it: these are relationship decisions with consequences beyond the order. The same holds for anything customer facing, because an acknowledgement and a confirmed date are commitments. The agent drafts, a named person releases, and the system enforces that rather than a policy document.

Where to start

Start with intake and the checking layer, not with confirmation. Let the agent read every incoming order, produce the structured draft, run the part number translation, the unit of measure check and the price variance, and hand a person a pre-checked order to approve. That alone removes most of the keying and catches the errors that currently surface at despatch. Change orders come next, because they are the biggest hidden cost and the least structured. Leave date confirmation until your lead time data has been reviewed, and keep anything that commits you to a customer behind a human release until the output has earned trust on your awkward accounts.
