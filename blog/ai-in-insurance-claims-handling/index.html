AI in Insurance Claims Handling: From First Notice to Settlement

If you run a claims organisation, you already know where the time goes. It is not the hard decisions. It is everything around them. An adjuster with a caseload of open files spends most of the day reading emails, chasing missing documents, rekeying details from a loss report into the claims platform, and writing the same status update for the fourth time. Cycle times stretch, loss adjustment expense creeps up, and your most experienced people spend their judgement on triage instead of on the claims that genuinely need it.

Production AI agents change that arithmetic, but only when they are wired into the systems you already run. An agent that lives in a chat window and summarises documents on request is a demo. An agent that reads the claim file in your core system, makes a bounded decision, writes its output back as structured data, and escalates to a named adjuster when it is unsure is an operational tool. This article walks through that lifecycle stage by stage, including the points where the agent must stop and hand over.

Structured intake at first notice of loss

First notice of loss arrives in every format your policyholders can invent. Web forms, call transcripts, broker emails, photos of handwritten forms, voicemails. Someone has to turn that into a claim record, and today that someone is usually a person copying fields.

An intake agent reads the raw notification, whatever the channel, and extracts the facts your claim record needs: policy number, insured party, date and location of loss, peril, parties involved, injuries reported, and a plain summary of what happened. It checks the extracted policy number against your policy administration system, confirms the policy was in force on the date of loss, and flags mismatches such as a lapsed policy or a name that does not match the policyholder.

What it writes back is a fully populated claim record in your core claims system, with each field tagged with a confidence level and a link to the passage or image it came from. What it decides is narrow: whether the notification contains enough to open a claim, and which fields need clarification. Where a field is ambiguous it drafts a clarification question rather than guessing. When the notification describes an injury, a fatality, or a distressed claimant, it routes straight to a human, because empathy at first contact is not a task to automate.

Coverage checks and severity triage

Once a claim exists, someone has to answer two questions quickly: is this likely covered, and how much attention does it need? Both are reading problems before they are judgement problems.

A triage agent reads the claim facts alongside the actual policy wording, schedules, and endorsements for that specific policy, not a generic product summary. It maps the reported loss against covered perils, exclusions, limits, and deductibles, and produces a coverage view: which sections appear to respond, which exclusions might apply, and which policy language drives each conclusion, quoted and cited. It also assigns a provisional severity and complexity band based on the peril, the reported damage, and the presence of injuries or third parties.

The write back is a coverage memo attached to the claim file and a routing decision in your workflow tool: straight through processing for simple, clearly covered, low value claims where you have authorised it, or assignment to the right adjuster queue by severity and specialism. The handover rule matters most here. The agent never issues a denial. Anything that looks like a coverage problem is packaged as a recommendation with cited policy language and sent to an adjuster, because a wrongly declined claim costs you far more than the minutes the agent saved.

Reading the document pile

A moderately complex claim accumulates a pile of paper: repair estimates, medical reports, police reports, invoices, photographs, expert opinions, correspondence. Adjusters do not lack the skill to read these. They lack the hours.

A document agent processes every inbound document as it lands in your document management system. It classifies the document type, extracts the structured facts, and reconciles them against what the claim file already says. A repair estimate gets its line items extracted and compared against typical costs for that kind of damage. A police report gets checked against the claimant's own account, and discrepancies in date, location, or description are flagged rather than silently absorbed.

What the agent writes back is a running, current summary of the claim on the file itself, plus structured extractions in the fields your downstream reporting depends on. What it decides is whether each document is consistent with the file. What it hands over is every inconsistency, with both versions side by side so the adjuster starts the conversation informed instead of surprised. The practical effect is that when an adjuster opens a file, the reading is already done and the open questions are already listed.

Keeping claimants informed

Most inbound calls to a claims team ask one question: what is happening with my claim? Every one of those calls interrupts an adjuster, and the answer usually exists in the system already.

A communication agent watches claim status transitions in your core system and drafts the next update to the claimant: what has happened, what happens next, what is needed from them and by when. It reads the claim file so the update is specific rather than templated, and it writes every message back to the file, a single record of what the claimant has been told. It can also read inbound replies and file the documents claimants send to the right place.

The boundary you should hold: the agent never communicates a coverage position, a settlement figure, or anything with legal weight. Those messages are drafted for the adjuster to review and send. And any inbound message showing distress, a complaint, or a threat of legal action goes to a human immediately.

Spotting recovery and subrogation early

Subrogation is where money leaks quietly. The signals that another party should pay are usually present in the file from early on, in a police report, a liability admission in an email, a product fault noted in a repair estimate. They get missed because nobody reads the whole file with recovery in mind.

A recovery agent does exactly that read, continuously. It looks for third party liability indicators across every document on the file and scores the recovery potential. When it finds a signal, it writes a subrogation referral into your workflow with the evidence attached: the documents, the passages, and why recovery looks viable. It decides only whether a referral is worth a specialist's time. The pursuit itself, the negotiation with the other insurer, the decision to litigate, stays entirely with your recovery team. The value is that referrals arrive while the claim is still open and evidence is still fresh, not in a file review months after settlement.

Preparing the settlement file

By the time a claim is ready to settle, the adjuster needs the whole picture in one place: verified damages, applicable limits and deductibles, prior payments, reserve history, and the reasoning that supports the number. Assembling that picture is hours of work per claim.

A settlement agent builds it. It reads the complete file, reconciles every verified cost against coverage, applies deductibles and limits from the policy, and produces a draft settlement summary with a proposed figure and an itemised basis for it. It checks the figure against your authority thresholds and states whether the adjuster on the file can approve it or must refer it upward. It writes the draft summary and the itemisation back to the claim file as the working settlement document.

The decision to settle is never the agent's. The adjuster reviews the basis, adjusts the figure with judgement the file cannot capture, and owns the outcome. What the agent has removed is the assembly work, so settlement conversations start earlier, on a file that is actually complete.

Where to start

Do not start with settlement, and do not start with fraud. Start where the work is high volume, low ambiguity, and easy to check. In most claims organisations that is intake extraction or document processing. Both have a clear ground truth, because you can compare the agent's extraction against what a person would have keyed, and both fail safely, because a wrong extraction is caught at the next human touchpoint rather than in a payment.

Run it in shadow mode first, producing output alongside your existing process without acting on anything, and measure agreement with your own people. Give it production access only when the numbers justify it, and then behind explicit authority limits. Log every decision with its inputs and reasoning, in a form your auditors and regulators can read. One workflow, measured honestly, in production, teaches you more than a dozen pilots.

Where AI fails here

You should hear this from a firm that builds these systems. AI agents fail in claims in predictable ways, and pretending otherwise is how programmes die.

They fail on judgement under genuine ambiguity. A policy wording dispute, a credibility assessment, a claimant whose account has shifted for innocent reasons, these require experience an agent does not have. They fail on empathy, and claims is an empathy business at its worst moments. They inherit whatever bias sits in your historical data, which is why any model touching fraud signals or triage needs testing for disparate outcomes before and after deployment, not assurances. They fail quietly on poor inputs: a blurry photo or a badly scanned form produces a confident wrong extraction unless confidence thresholds are enforced ruthlessly. And they fail organisationally when they are deployed as black boxes, because the first time an adjuster cannot see why the agent concluded something, trust is gone and the tool is routed around.

The honest posture is narrow scopes, cited reasoning, hard authority limits, human ownership of every consequential decision, and continuous measurement against human baselines. Agents built that way earn their place in a regulated process. Agents built as oracles do not.

Claims handling rewards what production agents are good at: reading everything, keeping records straight, drafting the routine, and surfacing the exceptions, at volume, without fatigue. It punishes what they are bad at, which is why the design question is never whether a human stays in the loop but where the loop is drawn.
