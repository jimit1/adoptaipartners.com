AI for Data Entry in Healthcare

23 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A biller opens Tuesday's theatre log. The surgeon wrote the implant into the free text of the operation note, the peel off label is stuck to the paper sheet, and the billing system wants an item, a quantity, a lot reference and a charge line. She keys what she can read and leaves the rest for the theatre manager.

That desk is the subject here: the back office that maintains a provider organisation's record spine. The front desk end, registration and insurance capture, is its own subject. This is the keying done after the patient goes home, to make the clinical record, the billing system and the master files agree with each other and with what happened.

The same patient under more than one record number

A person reaches your organisation by several routes, and each route can create them again. An emergency attendance under a shortened first name, a referral letter with the surname spelled as it sounds, a birth date transposed at a portal, a married name where the last record held a maiden one. None of it looks wrong at the moment of creation.

The cost lands later and lands clinically: allergies on one record and results on the other, an account that should show a balance showing nothing.

Merging is close to irreversible, because downstream systems have already consumed the number about to disappear. So an agent gets wide latitude to propose candidate pairs with evidence attached, scoring name, birth date, address history and shared episodes, and no authority at all to merge.

The overlay, which is worse than the duplicate

The opposite error is two people sharing one record. An agent is useful at surfacing overlay candidates, because they leave contradictions a machine can see: two blood groups, or care recorded in two places on the same afternoon. Treat that report as a safety output and give it an owner.

Charge entry and the gap between what was done and what was keyed

Revenue leaks where activity becomes a charge line. The second procedure in the same session never reaches the sheet. The drug given in the chair sits in the notes and nowhere in the charge file.

The useful comparison is between the clinical documentation and the charges raised for the same episode. An agent reads the operative note, the medication record and the theatre log, proposes the lines that appear to be missing, and quotes the sentence it read them from. It works in reverse too, flagging charges with nothing documented behind them.

Which code describes an episode is clinical coding and belongs to another team. Whether a charge exists at all, and whether quantity, site and operator match the note, is keying. Anything that changes money is confirmed by a person.

Rate tables that are wrong from the day they load

Contracted rates arrive as an annexe to an agreement and are loaded by hand: a long schedule of items, rates, effective dates and carve outs, keyed under time pressure with no acknowledgement step. The system simply begins believing the table.

From then on the error is invisible, because expected value is calculated from the same wrong number that will be used to check the payment. Underpayments go undetected because nothing in the organisation disagrees with them.

An agent reads the executed schedule against the loaded table and produces a difference list: lines in one and not the other, values that disagree, dates that do not match. Negotiating the rate is contract work.

Posting a remittance line by line

Most electronic remittance posts itself, so what is left on the desk is the awkward residue: paper advices and a cheque with a stub listing accounts in a layout unique to that payer.

The temptation under pressure is to post at claim level, which destroys the part that matters. A line carries a payment, a contractual adjustment, a withhold, an amount moved to the patient and a reason for each. Post the total and the organisation can no longer say why it was paid what it was paid.

An agent can read a paper advice into proposed line level postings and queue what it cannot match to an open episode. Misposting is the quieter failure: cash settles on the wrong episode, or on a different patient entirely, and only one account complains. Nothing moves a posted payment without a person approving it.

Provider records that have to agree everywhere

Each clinician carries a national identifier, a licence record, a billing group, site affiliations and effective dates, and those values must match across the practice system, the billing system and every payer portal. They drift the moment anything changes, and weeks later claims reject for reasons that never mention provider data.

Verifying credentials and granting privileges is a governance process with its own page. An agent can hold a continuous comparison across every system it can read, report the fields that disagree, and prepare the change list for each portal. Portals rarely offer an interface, so a person keys the change and the agent reads the portal back to confirm what was saved.

Supplies, implants and the theatre log

Consumable and implant usage is captured on paper at the point of use, then keyed twice over: once for the charge, once for the traceability record that must be searchable if a device is recalled.

The source is a handwritten sheet and a set of peel off labels. An agent reads labels into structured lines and matches them against the item master. It must never invent an item, because a plausible guess corrupts stock and traceability together.

Documents, messages and the queue nobody owns

Results, letters and discharge summaries arrive continuously, including on paper, and somebody turns the values inside them into discrete fields. Filing is the risk rather than reading, because a result filed to the wrong person is a clinical incident rather than a keying error, so the agent proposes values with the source region attached and cannot file to a patient it is unsure about.

The automated path fails differently. Messages reject one at a time, for a code the receiver does not hold or a field longer than the destination allows, and collect in a queue whose owner left. Somebody then keys the missing information by hand while the message sits there, so replaying the backlog creates the entry twice. The data exists twice and agrees once.

Group failures by cause rather than by timestamp, propose a correction per group, and identify which have already been fixed by hand. A person authorises the replay.

Returns, and the system that was never switched off

Mandatory returns, registry submissions and funder reports are usually built by one person from extracts and a spreadsheet of manual adjustments. Some required fields exist nowhere in any system, so they are filled from memory and from last time's file. An agent can assemble the extract, reconcile every figure to a source, and mark which fields are derived, which are carried forward and which have no source at all. Signing the submission stays with a person.

Part of the difficulty is that almost every provider runs a system that was replaced but never retired, with staff keying into both. A continuous comparison across the two, reporting records that disagree and fields that drift, is the cheapest thing here to deploy because it writes nothing anywhere, and it produces the evidence needed to finally retire the second system.

What may post itself and what must wait for a person

Confidence is not the right axis on its own. Consequence is. A corrected street name and a merged patient record are not the same decision, and one threshold across both is how organisations end up trusting automation exactly where they should not.

Set the bar per field. Descriptive fields can post at ordinary confidence. Anything that merges records, moves cash, changes a rate, alters a clinical value or touches provider identity queues for a person, permanently, not only during the first phase. Start with everything in a queue anyway, because reviewing a prefilled record is faster than keying one.

Log the source document, the region, the confidence and the approver for every field written, and never let automation overwrite a value a human has corrected.

Where AI fails here

Identity resolution defeats it on the cases that matter: twins sharing a birth date, an address and a surname, or a parent and child with the same name. It can propose forever and still be wrong precisely where the consequence is clinical, which is why merge authority stays with a person.

Confidence does not track legibility. A lot number read from a photographed sticker at an angle, a fax degraded through two generations: the model returns a value and feels no less certain about it. Treat traceability fields as exceptions rather than as extraction.

Contradictions between sources are not solvable by reading harder. When the operative note and the theatre log disagree about what was used, somebody has to ask the theatre, and an agent that picks the more plausible option has manufactured a fact.

Silent wrong keying is worse than a queue. A queue is visible and gets worked. A confidently posted wrong rate, wrong account or wrong result propagates into money and into care, and is found months later by whatever depends on it.

Local convention stalls this work more often than model quality does. If the rules for which system wins and which name is canonical live in the heads of two long serving staff, writing them down is the first half of the project.

Which part of the record spine to fix first

Start where the agent writes nothing: a duplicate candidate report, a comparison of the loaded rate table against the signed schedule, a divergence report across the system you never retired. Then charge gap review, which proposes but does not post, and paper remittance into a review queue. Merges and cash corrections come last and stay supervised.

You do not need a pilot, because the test set already exists. Take records your staff keyed and later corrected, run the agent against the original documents in shadow, and compare its output against both the first keying and the correction. Look hardest at the records that were argued over, because agreement on the easy ones tells you nothing.

Measure the manual baseline first: rekeying volume, duplicates created each month, unposted cash, claims rejected for provider or rate reasons.
