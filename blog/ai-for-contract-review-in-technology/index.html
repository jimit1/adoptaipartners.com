AI for Contract Review in Technology Companies: Inbound Paper and Outbound Redlines

16 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

A software company runs two contract queues at once. Inbound: vendor agreements, supplier paper, procurement portals, the terms attached to every tool an engineering team wants this week. Outbound: your own standard agreement going to a customer and coming back as tracked changes with someone else's positions written into it.

In house legal sits between them, a small function by design, and between a sales team that wants the deal signed before the quarter closes and a security team whose answer to anything unusual is no. Both pressures arrive in the same week.

What makes in house review different

Nobody is billing for this work. Review is overhead, visible only when it slows a deal down, and volume clusters at quarter ends. Unlike a firm reviewing paper as a service, you see the same few documents over and over, so little of the queue is novel legal analysis. It is reading a document you have effectively read before and spotting where it differs from a position you already decided.

Inbound vendor paper and the portal it arrives through

Inbound is where the volume is, mostly low stakes with a few genuinely dangerous items hidden in it. An agent can carry the first pass.

It reads the agreement in full, maps the operative clauses, and compares each against the positions your team has settled: liability, indemnities, data handling, termination, assignment, audit. For each it reports what the contract says, what your position requires and the gap, quoting rather than paraphrasing. It also reports what is absent, because a missing limitation of liability is usually the costlier problem.

Two checks are specific to a software business. Whether the vendor will hold anything belonging to your customers, which turns a tool purchase into something your own customer contracts have views about. And whether the vendor may use your data to train their models, often missed because it sits under an acceptable use heading.

Procurement portals and supplier questionnaires

The other half of inbound is not a contract at all. It is an onboarding portal wanting your insurance evidence, entity details, policy documents and standard answers to questions you have answered many times before.

An agent maintaining an approved answer library drafts the submission, quotes the source for each answer, and flags anything without a strong match as genuinely new. It never invents a control and never upgrades a partial answer into a yes. The submission is assembly work. The sign off is not.

Outbound redlines against a playbook

The redline arrives as a tracked changes document, often late, often from counsel on the other side who does this full time, and the sales owner wants a view before the next call.

An agent compares the returned document against your own executed template rather than reading it fresh. That comparison is the point. It identifies every substantive change, separates drafting tidy ups from the ones that move risk, and classifies each against your playbook. Where a fallback exists it proposes it. Where none exists it flags and stops.

Standard, fallback and walk away

Most companies have no playbook written down. What they have is a general counsel who knows which cap is fine, which indemnity is tradeable and which clause has never been conceded. That works until two deals arrive at once.

Writing three positions down per clause, what you offer as standard, what you accept as a fallback, and where you stop, is the first real project. It is worth doing even if you never deploy an agent, because it lets other people answer questions that currently queue behind one person.

The order form is where the contract actually changes

Redlines to the master agreement get read carefully. The order form often does not, and it is the document sales is most likely to edit directly, sometimes after review.

An agent checking each outbound order form against the approved template catches what matters: a renewal mechanic differing from the master, a payment term nobody approved, a bespoke service level promised in a notes field, a special condition overriding the general terms it sits under.

The security schedule and the data processing terms

These documents get negotiated by people whose job is not to know what your product does, which is how a company commits to something its architecture does not support.

An agent compares the security schedule a customer wants you to sign against the one your team approved, and against answers you have already given in questionnaires. If you told one customer data stays in a particular processing location and are about to sign a schedule saying otherwise, surface that before signature, not during an incident.

Data processing terms follow the same pattern. The agent extracts specifics rather than summarising: permitted purposes, deletion obligations, incident notification duties, audit rights and the subprocessor approval mechanism. That last one is an operational trap. Engineering adds an infrastructure provider, and somewhere in the portfolio is a contract requiring prior notice. Nobody outside legal connects those events unless a system does.

Uptime, service credits and what support you promised

Service commitments are negotiated on a curve. The standard applies to most customers, larger ones extracted more, and a few have something bespoke agreed years ago by someone who has left.

An agent extracting the availability structure across the portfolio gives you the picture: the committed level per customer, how availability is measured and what is excluded, the credit mechanism and whether credits are the sole remedy. When an outage happens, who is entitled to what should be an extraction, not an evening opening files.

Intellectual property, feedback and model or data usage

Your agreement probably lets you use customer feedback freely, and says what you may do with the data customers put into the product. Both are heavily negotiated, and both are where a concession made to close one deal constrains the product for everyone.

An agent checks each outbound redline for changes to these clauses at high severity: narrowing of your rights over aggregated or de-identified data, restrictions on using customer data to improve the service, ownership language attaching to model outputs. Inbound, the same check runs over components your product embeds, including licence obligations on open source you ship rather than merely use.

What did we already agree to, and when does it renew

Reviewing a contract and answering a question about one are different jobs, and the second is often the more valuable half. Sales asks whether a prospect's affiliate is covered. Finance asks who has uncapped renewal pricing. Security asks who must be notified about a subprocessor change. An acquirer's counsel asks which agreements restrict change of control.

Today a person answers those by reading signed documents, so they get asked less often than they should. An agent working from an extracted, cited contract register answers them in the time it takes to ask, and shows the clause it relied on. The discipline that makes this safe is version control: answer from the executed document plus its amendments and order forms.

The same register carries the dates. Auto renewal windows on both sides, notice periods, price escalation rights you hold and never exercise, expiry of the vendor agreements production depends on. An agent raises each with the named owner while there is still time to act, and states the consequence of doing nothing. It never serves a notice itself.

Evaluating it against contracts you have already negotiated

Do not evaluate this on sample documents. Take agreements your team negotiated recently, where you know what was flagged, conceded and missed, and run the agent over them blind.

You are looking for three things. Did it find what your reviewer found. Did it find anything your reviewer missed, which happens more often than teams expect on long schedules. And what did it raise that was noise, because a system that flags everything is one people learn to click through.

Where AI fails here

The most common failure is not the model. It is that the playbook does not exist in writing, so there is nothing to check against. An agent given no positions produces something that sounds like one, which is worse than silence.

Commercial judgement is not delegable. Whether to concede a clause to close a strategic account is a business decision, not a legal one, and it turns on leverage and appetite no document contains. The agent can say what the deviation is, not whether to take it.

Clauses that read fine alone can be dangerous beside another clause elsewhere. An acceptable cap next to a broad indemnity, or a service credit regime no longer the sole remedy because a termination right sits in a schedule, are interaction problems models miss because they reason clause by clause.

Non standard paper defeats the comparison. When a counterparty sends a template the agent has never seen, its confidence does not drop the way a lawyer's would. Route unfamiliar structures to a human by rule.

Confident redlines can quietly change meaning. An edit that reads cleanly and is grammatically better can shift an obligation from reasonable efforts to absolute, or widen a defined term. Every proposed edit needs reading as an edit, in context.

Version errors are the quiet one. An agent answering from a superseded master, or from the template rather than the executed copy, gives a fluent wrong answer nobody checks because the format looks authoritative. Cite the document version on every answer.

And the obvious one: nobody should sign anything because a model called it market standard. The model has no view of your risk appetite, no professional responsibility, and no way to know what your business can deliver.

Where to start

Start with extraction, not negotiation. Build the register from your executed customer agreements, their order forms and amendments, with a citation on every field, and have someone check a sample. You will find renewal dates, bespoke service levels and data commitments nobody currently knows about.

Write the playbook down second. Then put the agent on inbound vendor paper, where volume is high and a human catching an error costs nothing, before you let it near a customer redline in the closing week of a quarter.
