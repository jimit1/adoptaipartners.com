AI for Quality Control Documentation in Energy

14 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

The plant is mechanically complete. The commissioning team has pressure tested, flushed, dried and energised. What is holding the handover is a dossier. In it, a radiographic report references a weld absent from the current weld map, a material test certificate is missing for a spool in a line already insulated, and a witness signature on an inspection and test plan is dated after the activity it was meant to witness. Three people are working through a shared folder establishing which of those is clerical and which is real.

This happens on refinery turnarounds, gas compression packages, wind farm balance of plant, substations and new build generation. The engineering finishes. The evidence that it was done correctly takes longer, for reasons unrelated to engineering judgement.

Energisation waits on a file, not on a plant

The handover dossier is a deliverable with a payment term attached, and almost every project treats it as administration. It is assembled at the end, from material created over years by people who have gone to other jobs.

It is late for a structural reason. Assembling it means reconciling thousands of documents, and nobody can start until most of them exist. So the one task that could have run continuously gets compressed into the window where the client is already asking why the asset is not in service.

Contractor packs arrive in whatever shape the contractor keeps them

The fabrication shop sends scanned certificates with handwritten annotations. The electrical subcontractor exports from its own system with different field names. The instrument vendor sends one bound file with no index.

An agent can normalise these on arrival instead of at handover. It reads each pack, identifies what document type every item actually is regardless of file name, extracts the references the record turns on, and writes those into your register against the right tag, line or circuit. The pack stays as submitted. It becomes searchable the week it lands.

Weld maps and the joint that carries three different numbers

A weld has an identity on the isometric, another in the fabricator's traveller, and sometimes a third in the inspection report, because the radiographer wrote what was chalked on the pipe. Reconciling them by hand is what weld engineers spend their weeks doing, and it is the usual source of a gap that is not one.

An agent reads the weld map, the welding log and the inspection reports together and proposes the matches, with a confidence on each and the unmatched remainder listed plainly. It flags the joints that matter: no examination record on a line that required coverage, a repair weld never re-examined, a welder named on a joint outside a current qualification.

Radiographs read against the procedure they were shot under

A radiographic or ultrasonic report is only evidence if the examination was performed to the right procedure, by a technician with a current certification, at the correct stage, with the acceptance criteria the code demands for that service. Those facts live in four places, so reviewers check a sample.

An agent can check them all. It resolves the procedure revision in force on the date of the shot, confirms the technician was certified and at the right level, and compares the reported acceptance criteria against the specification for that line class. What it cannot resolve goes to a reviewer, who then spends the review on judgement rather than arithmetic.

Heat numbers, and the spool that could be one of four

Traceability here means saying that this installed component came from this heat of steel, certified to the grade the specification required. The chain runs from mill certificate to purchase order to receiving inspection to spool drawing to installed tag, and it breaks where someone cut a length off a stock item and did not record which.

An agent given the certificates, receiving records and fabrication drawings builds and maintains that chain, then reports where it cannot close. A declared gap is manageable: a positive material identification test while access exists. The same gap found during a certifying body audit after insulation is a different conversation.

Witness and hold points, and the notice nobody sent

An inspection and test plan assigns intervention points to the contractor, the client representative and sometimes a notified body. A hold point means work stops until the holder attends. The failure mode is quiet. The activity proceeds because the notice went to someone who had left, and the record shows a signature obtained later against work nobody independently saw.

An agent can run the plan as a live schedule rather than a document. It tracks completion against progress and inspection records, raises the notification with the required notice period, and marks the attendance actually obtained. Where a hold point was passed without the holder present, it says so at the time.

Factory acceptance, then the same test again on site

Factory acceptance records for a switchgear panel or turbine auxiliary skid come in the vendor's own format, reviewed by whoever is available. The site acceptance test repeats much of it after transport and installation, and the question that matters is whether any result moved.

An agent compares the two parameter by parameter and lists the deviations with the tolerance applied. It also does the part that gets skipped: checking that every punch item raised at the factory was cleared before shipment or carried into the site list, rather than lost between two companies' spreadsheets.

A disposition written in one line by someone now demobilised

Non-conformance reports are where quality documentation earns its cost or fails to. The report itself is usually adequate. The disposition is where it goes wrong: "accept as is" with no technical justification, a repair recorded without its procedure, a concession granted verbally and written up later, or an NCR left open because the person who could close it moved on.

An agent maintains the population properly. It drafts the report from the inspection finding with affected tags, quantities and drawings resolved, groups prior reports with the same signature so a recurring defect looks like one, and tracks every open item against its owner and age. It can say which open NCRs sit on systems about to be handed over, which is the list nobody has on the day they need it.

Punch items that get closed on a promise

At commissioning the punch list becomes the live document, split into categories that determine whether the asset can be taken over. Items are raised by several parties, often as a photograph with a sentence of description, and closed by assertion far more often than by evidence.

An agent can take that intake in whatever form it arrives, write a structured item with the system and tag identified, categorise it against your own rules, and spot the same defect raised by three disciplines. On closure it checks that the evidence attached corresponds to the item rather than merely existing.

The gauge that was out of calibration in April

Every pressure test, torque figure, loop check and insulation resistance reading came from an instrument, and that instrument needs a calibration certificate valid on the date of the reading, traceable to a national standard. Reviewers spot check it. The expensive discovery is a test instrument whose certificate had lapsed, with weeks of readings behind it.

An agent checks it exhaustively, cross referencing every test record against the calibration status of the instrument named on it on that date, and listing readings taken on lapsed equipment while retesting is cheap. If your records do not name the instrument at all, that is worth knowing early too.

Marked up prints that never reached the issued drawing

Field changes get written on a print, and someone is meant to transmit those redlines to engineering so the as built drawing reflects the asset. On a busy site a proportion never make the trip, and the dossier then holds a drawing that disagrees with the plant. An agent compares the marked up set against the issued revision and the test records, then reports the contradictions: a valve witnessed on a test but absent from the drawing, a cable schedule showing a route the installation record does not support.

A narrow question years after the team has dispersed

Years later a regulator, an insurer, a certifying body or a buyer asks a narrow question about one component. The team is dispersed. What remains is the dossier.

Daily reports become the only witness left

Wellsite and turbine daily reports, shift logs and commissioning diaries are written for the next morning's meeting, yet they are often the only contemporaneous account of why something was done a particular way. An agent that indexes them against tags, systems and dates turns narrative into evidence, and can retrieve the entries bearing on a question nobody anticipated when they were written.

Where AI fails here

Disposition of a non-conformance is not a drafting task. Accepting material as is, granting a concession or agreeing a waiver against a specification carries engineering liability, and the record needs the name of the qualified person who made that judgement. An agent that assembles evidence and lays out options is useful. One that chooses between them is a liability you cannot insure.

Nobody can delegate a certifying statement. When a responsible engineer signs that a system was constructed and tested in accordance with the applicable code, that signature means a person examined the evidence. The agent's job is to make the evidence complete and the contradictions visible before that examination, not to shorten it.

Code interpretation stays human. Whether a weld repair exceeded what the code allows, or whether a deviation is material, is an argument between qualified people, and an agent that writes fluently will make a weak reading sound settled.

Source quality sets the ceiling. Poor scans, handwritten travellers, tag numbering that changed between front end engineering and construction, and vendor packs with no consistent identifier all produce confident wrong matches. Build the workflow so unmatched stays a visible output rather than something quietly resolved to the nearest candidate.

Where to start

Pick one package on one asset where the evidence exists and the answer is checkable. Weld record reconciliation is a good first choice: you can test it against a pack your engineers have closed out, weighing gaps it found that they missed against gaps it claimed that were not real.

Decide in writing what the agent may read, what it may write into your quality register, and which outputs always route to a named reviewer. Keep disposition, concession and certification outside its reach from the first day.

Measure something your project controls function already reports, such as open non-conformances on systems scheduled for handover.
