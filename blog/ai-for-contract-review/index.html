AI for contract review: what legal teams can safely hand to an agent

If you run a legal team, you know the shape of the problem. The business sends over another vendor agreement at 4pm, sales wants an NDA turned around before a call tomorrow morning, and procurement has a renewal queue nobody has looked at since the person who owned it left. Most of this work is not hard law. It is reading, checking, comparing and chasing. It is also the work that fills your team's week and pushes the genuinely difficult questions to the evenings.

An AI agent will not replace legal judgement, and you should be suspicious of anyone who says it will. What it can do is take on the structured, repeatable layers of contract review, the parts where the answer is already written down in your playbook, and hand the rest to a lawyer with the groundwork done. The distinction that matters is not "AI or human". It is which specific steps you can safely delegate, under what supervision, and where the handover line sits.

First-pass NDA review against your standard positions

NDAs are the classic starting point because the risk surface is narrow and your positions are usually settled. An agent set up for NDA review reads the incoming document in full, identifies the operative clauses (definition of confidential information, term, permitted disclosures, residuals, non-solicitation, governing law) and compares each one against the positions your team has approved.

Concretely, the agent extracts each clause, matches it to the corresponding playbook entry, and classifies it as acceptable, acceptable with a standard fallback, or outside policy. Where a fallback exists, it can propose the redline in your house language. Where the counterparty has inserted something your playbook does not cover at all, an unusual injunction clause, say, or an indemnity hiding inside an NDA, it does not guess. It flags the clause, quotes it verbatim, and routes the document to a lawyer with a note explaining what it could not resolve.

The result is that most mutual NDAs go from inbox to signature-ready with a human doing a two-minute confirmation rather than a forty-minute read. The unusual ones still reach a lawyer, but they arrive pre-analysed.

Playbook comparison on third party paper

The same pattern extends to heavier documents: MSAs, SaaS agreements, DPAs, supply contracts arriving on the counterparty's template. Here the agent's job is bigger but structurally identical. It reads the full document, builds a clause map, and checks each provision against your negotiation playbook: liability caps and carve-outs, indemnities, IP ownership and licence grants, termination rights, payment terms, audit rights, insurance requirements.

For each clause the agent records three things: what the contract actually says, what your playbook requires, and the gap between them. It then produces an issues list ordered by severity, with the exact contract language quoted so a lawyer never has to trust a paraphrase. Well-configured agents also check for what is missing, because the most dangerous clause in third party paper is often the one that is not there, such as an absent limitation of liability or a missing data protection schedule.

The handover rule is firm. The agent proposes redlines only where your playbook already contains an approved fallback. Anything requiring a judgement call, a bespoke carve-out, an unusual risk allocation, a commercial trade-off, goes to the lawyer as a flagged issue, not a suggested edit. The lawyer negotiates. The agent prepares.

Contract data extraction and repository clean-up

Ask most legal teams what their executed contracts say about renewal notice periods and the honest answer is that nobody fully knows. Agreements live in shared drives, email attachments and old CLM instances, and the metadata was either never captured or was typed in by hand years ago.

An extraction agent works through a repository document by document. From each executed contract it pulls the fields you define: parties, effective date, initial term, renewal mechanics, notice periods, price and payment terms, liability cap, governing law, assignment and change of control provisions, termination triggers. Each extracted value is stored alongside a citation to the clause it came from, so anyone can verify a field in seconds rather than re-reading the agreement.

This is safe to delegate because it is verifiable. The agent is not interpreting; it is locating and transcribing, and it marks any field it cannot find with confidence as "not located" rather than inventing a value. A person then spot-checks a sample and reviews the low-confidence items. Teams typically find that the review effort is a small fraction of doing the extraction manually, and the output becomes the foundation for everything else on this list.

Renewal and obligation monitoring after signature

Review does not end at signature, though for many teams the file effectively closes there. An agent watching a clean contract repository can do the boring part of post-signature management indefinitely and without fatigue.

It works from the extracted data: for every active agreement, it knows the renewal date, the notice window and who owns the relationship. As deadlines approach, it raises the item with the owner, summarises what the contract requires, and states the consequence of missing the window, for example an automatic twelve-month renewal at an uplifted price. It can do the same for embedded obligations such as annual insurance certificates or the security reviews a contract entitles you to.

The agent never terminates, renews or notifies a counterparty on its own. It surfaces the decision to a human at the moment the decision is live, with the relevant clause attached. The judgement stays with you; the calendar management does not.

Procurement intake and contract triage

Before any review happens, someone has to decide what kind of review a contract needs, and that routing step is itself a quiet time sink. An intake agent sits at the front of the queue. When a request arrives, it reads the document and the request context, classifies the contract type, checks the value and risk factors against your triage rules, and routes accordingly.

A low-value renewal on your own paper with no changes might be cleared for signature under existing delegated authority. A mid-tier vendor agreement goes to the playbook review lane described above. Anything touching personal data, regulated activity, unusual jurisdictions or high contract value gets escalated straight to a named lawyer with a summary of why. The agent also catches the practical problems that stall queues, such as missing schedules or unsigned prior versions.

None of this is legal analysis. It is disciplined sorting, done consistently, at any hour, and it means your lawyers open files that are actually ready to work.

Portfolio-wide questions you could never afford to ask

Some questions are only expensive because answering them means re-reading hundreds of contracts. Which agreements contain change of control provisions that a prospective acquirer would trigger? Which customer contracts commit you to a service level you no longer offer? Which supplier terms lack the audit right your new compliance framework assumes? Where did you agree to most favoured customer pricing?

With a repository an agent can read, these become questions you ask in the morning and verify in the afternoon. The agent searches across the full portfolio, returns every clause that matches, quotes the language, and notes the agreements where the answer is genuinely ambiguous. A lawyer then reviews the hits rather than the haystack. When regulations change or a transaction is on the table, this is the difference between a two-week scramble and a same-day answer.

Where to start

Resist the temptation to automate the hardest work first. The right first project has three properties: high volume, settled playbook positions, and low consequence when a human catches an error at the review step. For most teams that means NDAs or contract data extraction.

Before any agent touches a document, write the playbook down. Much of what your senior lawyers "just know" has never been recorded, and an agent can only be as disciplined as the rules you give it. This exercise pays for itself even if you stop there.

Then run the agent in shadow mode. For a few weeks, it reviews everything but a lawyer still does the full review independently, and you compare the outputs. You will learn precisely where the agent is reliable and where it is not. Only then do you let it work first and have the lawyer confirm, and you keep measuring. Escalation rates, override rates and turnaround times tell you when to widen the agent's remit and when to tighten it.

Where AI fails here

An honest accounting matters more in legal than anywhere else, so here is ours.

Agents fail at judgement under ambiguity. A model can tell you a liability cap deviates from your playbook. It cannot tell you whether this counterparty relationship justifies accepting the deviation. It has no view on your risk appetite, your negotiating leverage or the politics of the deal, and it should never be allowed to fake one.

Agents can misread documents. Scanned agreements with poor OCR, defined terms that shift meaning across a suite of related contracts, amendments that silently override the master: these are real failure modes. Language models can also state wrong things fluently and confidently, which is precisely why every extraction and every flag should carry a verbatim citation a human can check in seconds. A system whose outputs cannot be traced to the source text does not belong in a legal workflow.

Agents do not carry professional responsibility. Legal advice remains the lawyer's, and privilege, confidentiality and regulatory duties need to be designed into the deployment from the start, including where documents are processed and what the model provider may retain. These are solvable engineering and contractual questions, but they must actually be solved, not waved at.

The pattern across all of these is the same: the failure modes are manageable when the system is built to expose its work and escalate its doubts, and dangerous when it is built to look finished.

The realistic promise is not a legal team without lawyers. It is a legal team where lawyers spend their time on negotiation, judgement and counsel, while agents handle the reading, checking, extracting and chasing that currently swallows the week, with every conclusion traceable to the text it came from and every hard call routed to a person. We build these systems inside organisations that recognise the difference between automating judgement and automating preparation, and the teams that get this right start small, measure honestly and expand only where the evidence supports it.
