AI for quality control documentation in manufacturing

28 August 2026  ·  6 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

In a well-run plant, the product is usually fine. It is the paperwork that is late, incomplete or inconsistent. Batch records with a missing initial on page eleven. Certificates of analysis assembled by copying values between spreadsheets. Deviation reports that take three weeks to write and say less than the operator said in the first ten minutes. Supplier certificates filed unread until an audit finds the gap. Quality engineers who joined to solve problems spend their days as documentation clerks, and every hour of it is on the critical path, because product does not ship until the record is closed.

The documentation is not optional and nobody serious suggests thinning it. The opportunity is different: most quality documentation is transcription, collation and cross-checking of data that already exists in your MES, LIMS, ERP and instrument outputs. That is agent work. The judgement, disposition decisions, root cause, sign-off, stays with qualified people, who finally get their time back to do it properly.

Batch record review by exception

Reviewing a completed batch record means checking hundreds of entries against specifications and against each other: every step signed, every parameter in range, every time stamp plausible, every deviation referenced. An agent performs the full review the moment the record closes: it reads the executed record, electronic or scanned paper, verifies completeness, checks recorded values against the master batch record's limits, confirms calculations, and cross-references any deviations against the deviation log.

Clean records are summarised in a review report for the quality reviewer, who confirms and releases. Records with findings arrive with each finding located, page, step, field, and classified: missing entry, out-of-trend value, arithmetic error, uncross-referenced deviation. The human role shifts from reading everything to judging the exceptions, which is what review-by-exception was always supposed to mean before volume made it aspirational. Release cycle time drops because the review starts within minutes of batch closure instead of waiting in a pile.

Certificates of analysis, assembled not retyped

A certificate of analysis is a formatting exercise wrapped around data your LIMS already holds. An agent pulls the approved results for the batch, checks each against the customer-specific specification, not just your internal one, since customers differ, assembles the certificate in the agreed template, and stages it for quality sign-off. If a result is missing, pending or out of the customer's spec, it stops and says so, rather than shipping a certificate that triggers a complaint.

Inbound, the same pattern runs in reverse: the agent reads supplier certificates as they arrive, extracts the reported values, checks them against your material specifications and the purchase order, files the certificate against the receipt in the ERP, and flags nonconforming or missing parameters before the material is released to production. Supplier paperwork stops being filed unread, and incoming quality stops depending on whether anyone had time that day.

Deviation and nonconformance write-ups

The gap between what an operator observed and what the deviation report eventually says is where investigation quality dies. An agent can capture the event conversationally at the line, take the operator's plain description, ask the clarifying questions your procedure requires, what product, what step, what was observed, what was done immediately, and draft the deviation record in your QMS with the batch, equipment and material context pulled from the MES automatically.

It then assembles the investigation file: the batch's process data around the event window, recent deviations on the same line or material, relevant maintenance history, the applicable procedures. The investigator starts with a curated evidence pack instead of a blank template and five system logins. The agent can draft the chronology and suggest which established root-cause categories the evidence resembles, but causal conclusions, CAPA decisions and effectiveness checks belong to qualified people, and the record should show a named human made them.

Trending and the quiet drift

Most quality systems trend what regulation forces them to trend and miss the rest. An agent watching your LIMS and process historian can run statistical rules across every parameter continuously, flagging drifts and shifts long before a specification is breached: a moisture result creeping up over eight batches, a fill weight distribution widening on one line, a supplier's material slowly migrating towards a spec limit.

Each flag arrives as a case with the trend plotted, the affected batches listed and the possible next checks named. Out-of-trend investigations start weeks earlier, and your periodic product reviews assemble themselves from cases already documented rather than from a year-end trawl.

Audit preparation and document retrieval

Audits are a retrieval exam. An agent that has indexed your controlled documents, training records, batch records and CAPA system turns preparation from weeks into days: it assembles the standard audit binder, verifies every referenced document is at its effective revision, checks training matrices against current procedures, and surfaces the gaps, the SOP revised without a training update, the CAPA past due, before the auditor does.

During the audit itself, requests like "show me the batch record and release data for this lot" become minutes, not an afternoon of runners. Afterwards, the agent drafts the response file for each finding with the evidence attached, and tracks commitments to their due dates.

Change control paperwork

Change control fails in the impact assessment: which documents, specifications, validations and training does this change actually touch? An agent reads the proposed change and searches the controlled document set for every reference to the affected material, parameter, equipment or method, producing a candidate impact list with citations. It drafts the change record, routes it through the approval workflow, and afterwards verifies the execution: every impacted document revised, every training assignment completed, before the change closes. Humans decide whether the change is sound; the agent guarantees nothing referenced was forgotten.

Where to start

Start with certificate of analysis assembly or inbound supplier certificate checking. Both are contained, high-volume, and verifiable against ground truth your LIMS already holds, and neither touches disposition judgement. Batch record review is the biggest prize but should come second: run the agent in parallel with your human review for a defined period, compare findings, and use the comparison both to validate the agent and, uncomfortably often, to discover what tired human review had been missing. Anything in a GxP or otherwise regulated environment must go through your computerised system validation route, so involve your quality and validation people from day one, scope the agent's role as assisting a named human reviewer, and keep signatures human. Deviation drafting and audit preparation follow naturally once the read-only foundations are trusted.

Where AI fails here

Directly: an agent must never disposition product. Release, reject, rework, concession, these are decisions regulation and good sense assign to qualified people, and the agent's job is to make the evidence for that decision complete and fast, not to make the call. Root cause analysis is genuinely hard and an agent's fluent narrative can be worse than useless if it sounds convincing while being wrong; treat drafted chronologies as scaffolding, and keep investigators sceptical.

Scanned and handwritten records carry real extraction error rates, and in quality documentation a confidently misread number is more dangerous than a flagged unreadable one, so the agent must be tuned to escalate uncertainty, never to guess. Validation is a genuine cost: in regulated environments the agent, its prompts and its revision history become part of your quality system, with change control of their own, and pretending otherwise will fail an audit. Finally, culture: if operators learn the agent's flags feel like surveillance, data quality will get worse, not better. Introduce it as the thing that writes the paperwork so people can solve the problems, because that is what it is for.

Quality documentation exists so that anyone, later, can know what happened and trust it. Agents do not weaken that, done properly they strengthen it, every record complete, every check run at full coverage, every finding logged with its evidence, while your quality engineers go back to engineering quality.
