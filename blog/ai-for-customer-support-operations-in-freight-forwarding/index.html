AI for Customer Support Operations in Freight Forwarding: The Enquiry Desk Behind Every Shipment

25 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Almost nobody contacts a freight forwarder to say that everything went to plan. The service desk at a forwarder or a logistics service provider hears from shippers, consignees, overseas agents and carriers, and the message nearly always concerns a shipment that has already slipped, been held, been amended, or been invoiced in a way the customer did not expect.

That gives the desk a shape unlike most support functions. The answer to almost every question lives in the operation, not in a knowledge base, and nothing useful can be said without reading the file, its milestones and its correspondence. Automation that cannot reach the operational record is limited to acknowledging receipt, which is the one thing customers already have plenty of.

Where is my shipment, and the three systems that have to agree

The most common question on the desk has no single source. Part of the answer sits in the carrier or airline milestone feed, part in the forwarder's own event records, part in the haulier or terminal record for the last leg. They disagree on timing, on vocabulary and sometimes on fact.

An AI system earns its place by reconciling those sources into one statement a coordinator would sign: which milestone is confirmed and which is projected, when each source was last refreshed, and where they conflict rather than picking the cheerful one. It also answers the question behind the question: whoever asks for status wants a date they can commit to further down their own chain.

Rollovers, offloads and the box that did not sail

The worst version of a status enquiry is the one where the customer knows before you do. A container is rolled to the next sailing, a consignment is offloaded for weight or space, a transhipment connection is missed, or the service changes upstream and nothing on the file says so.

These cases are hard because the signal is an absence. No load confirmation arrived, no departure event attached to the shipment, and a system watching only for events will never see it. The comparison has to run the other way: booked movement against actual, with an alert when the booked one passes without a matching record. The reply then needs the new routing, the revised arrival, the effect on any delivery appointment already made, and what the forwarder has done about it.

The exception notice that should have gone out first

A large share of this desk's volume is created by the desk. Every enquiry about an exception the forwarder already knew about is a notice that went out late or never left.

Inverting this is the most useful thing an AI system does here. It compares the file against what was promised: the committed arrival, the agreed delivery window, the routing. Where reality has moved, it drafts the notice with the reason, the revised expectation and the next action, and sends routine categories under rules the forwarder sets. Anything touching cost, liability or a sensitive account goes to a person. The measure worth watching is the share of exceptions where the customer heard from you first.

Free time, and the warning nobody sent

Free time runs on a clock the customer often does not know has started, and the first they hear of it is a charge they dispute. The events that start it are already in the system: discharge, availability, gate out, equipment return.

A system should count forward from those events, identify the party responsible under the agreed terms, and warn them before the last free day rather than after it. The warning has to say what stops the clock: an appointment booked, a release obtained, empty equipment returned. It should record that it was sent and to whom, because the argument later is always about whether anyone was told.

Amendments and late changes to a shipping instruction

Changes arrive as sentences in the middle of a thread. Add a reference for the consignee. The goods are now going to the second warehouse. Hold it for a week. None of these arrive as a form, and several arrive after the point where they are still free or possible.

The useful work is the feasibility check before anyone touches anything. Read the request against the state of the booking and the relevant cut off, then tell the customer what can still be changed, what now needs a new booking, and what carries a cost they should agree to first. Then hand the change on, restated as an instruction rather than a forwarded email.

Documents, releases and the proof of delivery chase

Much of document traffic is retrieval: the customer wants a copy of something that already sits on the file, or was sent it and lost the thread. That is containable, provided the system can locate the right version and confirm it belongs to the right party.

The rest is authority. Who may instruct release, whether the original is where it should be, whether charges are settled, whether the named party matches the instruction on file. Those decisions need a person or a rule the forwarder has written down. Proof of delivery chasing is different again: it runs outward to the haulier or the destination agent, and the value is in pursuing it on a schedule rather than waiting for a reminder.

A customs hold explained to someone who has never seen one

A shipper told their goods are held usually assumes they have done something wrong. The reply has to be calm and specific: what kind of check this is, which party is holding, what is needed and from whom, and what happens to time and cost while it sits.

An AI system drafts this from the hold record and from how the forwarder has explained the same situation before. What it must not do is predict the outcome, estimate how long a check will take, or offer an opinion on what the authority will decide. Give a time for the next update, not for resolution.

Enquiries that belong to pricing, credit or account management

Not everything that lands here should be answered here. A rate enquiry is a commercial commitment, and a figure offered by a service desk is one the company may not honour. The right behaviour is recognition and routing: capture the lane, commodity, volume, equipment and readiness date, hand pricing a brief rather than a forwarded thread.

Credit holds work the same way. When a shipment is not moving for a financial reason, the desk should acknowledge and route, not explain an account's credit position to whichever contact wrote in. Reporting requests, where a customer wants last month explained across all their shipments, are better assembled from the operational record than from memory.

A shortage report is the first page of a claim file

Damage and shortage arrive looking like ordinary email. Recognising them early matters more than answering them quickly, because the first exchange decides whether a claim is defensible later. Evidence disappears fast: photographs before the goods move, remarks on the delivery note, seal condition, who discovered it and when, and the notice deadlines running against the carrier.

A system should identify the message for what it is, ask for what is missing while it still exists, and assemble the file in the order the claims team needs it. What it must never do is assess liability, accept a claim, deny one, or use language that reads as acceptance.

Four parties, three time zones, and the hours nobody covers

One shipment can generate four conversations. The shipper chases from origin, the consignee from destination, the overseas agent in another format, and the carrier replies to something else entirely. Consolidating by shipment, so a coordinator opens one item with everything attached rather than four partial ones, is one of the biggest gains available.

Visibility is the constraint. What the consignee may see is not what the shipper may see, and agent correspondence can expose buying rates that must never reach a customer. That is a redaction problem before it is a routing problem. The same machinery covers the overnight and weekend gap, when a file sits untouched while the operation on the other side of the world keeps moving.

What containment means when every answer has an operational source

The honest measure is not how many enquiries avoided a person. It is how many received the answer a competent coordinator would have given. Sample the automated replies, have coordinators mark the ones they would have sent unchanged, and treat the rest as misses whether or not the customer complained.

Count a contact as contained only when it was answered without human touch and was not followed by a reopen, a chase or a call. Track proactive notices separately, because they remove volume rather than absorb it, and that is the more valuable number. The metric that flatters is fluency: a confident reply built on a stale milestone reads beautifully and costs you the call anyway.

Where AI fails here

The first limit is the record. A file is only as good as its last update, feeds go quiet exactly when something has gone wrong, and the absence of an event is the hardest signal to act on. A system that reads silence as normal will reassure customers while a container sits on a quay.

The second is commercial judgement. Whether to absorb a charge, what to say to an account in the middle of a tender, whether goodwill is owed after a bad month: none of that is in the data, and the answer rests on a relationship a coordinator carries in their head.

The third is the conversation that is no longer about a shipment. When a customer is angry about a pattern rather than a box, automating the reply makes it worse. Liability, claim outcomes and anything a lawyer might read aloud stay with people.

The fourth is written rules. A desk where escalation lives in individual habit cannot be automated cleanly, because the system inherits the ambiguity and applies it faster.

Proving it on one trade lane

Take a single lane, a single mode and a handful of customers whose traffic your team recognises on sight. Run the system in draft mode first, with every reply going out under a coordinator's name after they have read it.

The number that tells you whether it works is agreement: how often the coordinator sends the draft unchanged, and what they change when they do not. Read the corrections, because they are your escalation rules written down for the first time. Once a category agrees consistently, let it send directly and leave the rest in draft.
