AI use cases in energy trading and utilities back offices

28 August 2026  ·  6 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

The back office of a utility or an energy trading desk is a reconciliation machine. Trades confirmed against counterparty paperwork, scheduled volumes against metered actuals, settlement statements against internal calculations, billing determinants against tariff rules, and all of it against deadlines set by market operators and regulators who do not extend them. The people doing this work are experienced, careful and chronically stretched, because volumes grow with every new asset, every new market coupling and every retail customer, while headcount does not. Errors are rarely dramatic; they are quiet, a mispriced confirmation unnoticed for a month, a settlement variance written off because investigating it costs more than its face value, a regulatory submission assembled in a heroic weekend.

This is patient, rule-heavy, document-heavy work across ETRM systems, billing engines, market operator portals and shared drives. It is, in other words, the natural habitat of production AI agents. Here is where they fit.

Trade confirmation matching

Every trade generates paper: broker recaps, counterparty confirmations, exchange records. An agent reads each inbound confirmation, PDF, email body or platform download, extracts the economic terms, volume, price, delivery period, delivery point, option details where relevant, and matches them against the trade as captured in your ETRM. Perfect matches are confirmed and the trade record annotated. Discrepancies open an exception with both versions side by side and the differing term highlighted: a price off by a decimal, a delivery period shifted a day, a wrong index reference.

The agent drafts the query to the counterparty for a human to release, and tracks the exception to resolution. What changes is not the process, confirmation matching already exists, but the economics of thoroughness: every confirmation gets the same scrutiny within minutes of arrival, instead of the risk-weighted sampling a stretched team quietly falls back on.

Settlement and invoice reconciliation against market operators

Market operator settlement statements are long, dense and periodically wrong, and re-settlement windows mean each delivery day may be settled several times over months. An agent ingests each statement run, recalculates expected values from your own metered volumes, prices and charge rules, and lines the two up item by item. Variances within tolerance are logged and accepted. Variances beyond tolerance become cases: the agent gathers the relevant meter data, the applicable charge methodology and prior-run values, forms a hypothesis, our volume submission missed a revision, their loss factor changed, a unit conversion slipped, and drafts the dispute or query within the market's dispute window.

Because re-settlements are tracked run over run, nothing silently nets away. Teams discover the write-off habit was expensive: small variances, investigated cheaply by an agent, turn out to cluster into recoverable patterns.

Metering data quality and exception handling

Retail and grid billing both stand on metering data, and metering data arrives flawed: gaps, spikes, crossed channels, estimated reads that never get replaced by actuals. An agent works the validation exception queue in your meter data management system, classifying each failure and applying the estimation and substitution rules your market prescribes, documenting which rule it applied and why. Reads it cannot resolve within rules become field-work orders or retailer queries, raised in the right system with the evidence attached.

The same agent watches for the slow failures humans miss: a meter drifting against its history, a site whose consumption pattern changed shape in a way that suggests a wiring issue or theft rather than behaviour. Those become investigation cases with the anomaly plotted and the comparable history attached, for an engineer to judge.

Regulatory reporting and compliance evidence

Utilities file constantly: transaction reporting under market abuse and transparency regimes, outage and availability reporting, licence condition returns, ad hoc information requests from the regulator. An agent maintains the reporting calendar, assembles each submission from the systems of record, trades from the ETRM, outages from the asset management system, complaints metrics from the CRM, runs the validation rules the regulator publishes, and presents the completed return with a line-by-line source trail for a named human to review and submit.

The source trail is the point. When the regulator queries a figure eighteen months later, the answer is a lookup, not an archaeology project. And because the agent assembles continuously rather than at the deadline, gaps surface with weeks to fix them instead of hours.

Billing exceptions and tariff correctness

Retail billing engines throw exceptions daily: accounts that failed to bill, bills held for review because the amount looks implausible, tariff changes that did not land cleanly. An agent triages the held-bill queue, recalculating each bill independently from consumption and tariff rules. Where its calculation agrees with the engine and the amount is explained, an unusually cold month, a new appliance load pattern, a correctly applied price change, it releases the bill with the explanation logged. Where it disagrees, it has usually found the defect, a stale tariff assignment, a missed meter exchange, and routes the case with the diagnosis to a billing specialist.

The same recalculation logic, run across the billed base rather than the exception queue, becomes an assurance sweep: a periodic independent check that what went out the door matches the published tariffs. Mischarging findings and their remediations are exactly what regulators fine for; finding them yourself, early, is materially cheaper.

Counterparty and credit monitoring

Trading back offices track counterparty exposure against limits, collateral positions and contract terms. An agent keeps the file current: reads margin call notices and collateral statements as they arrive, reconciles them against internal exposure calculations, drafts responses to calls it agrees with and disputes for the ones it does not, and flags limit utilisation trends before they become breaches. Annual review packs, financials, news, payment behaviour from your own ledger, are assembled for the credit analyst rather than by the credit analyst. Humans keep every decision; they stop doing the collation.

Where to start

Start where the ground truth is arithmetic: settlement reconciliation or confirmation matching. Both have answers that are checkable to the penny, existing exception volumes you can baseline, and no customer-facing surface while the agent learns. Run in parallel with the current process for one settlement cycle, compare the agent's exception list against what your team caught, and you will have an honest accuracy figure and, usually, a list of variances the manual process was missing. Promote the mechanical resolutions to autonomous handling gradually, and keep human release on anything that leaves the organisation, disputes, regulatory returns, counterparty queries. Billing exception triage is a strong second phase; regulatory reporting assembly third, since it spans the most systems. Leave anything touching trading decisions or price forecasting out of scope entirely; that is a different discipline with a different risk profile, and mixing it into back-office automation muddies both.

Where AI fails here

Plainly: agents do not fix bad reference data, they expose it. The first month will surface delivery point mappings that were wrong for years and tariff tables with undocumented local exceptions, and someone must own that cleanup. Market rules change constantly, and an agent enforcing last quarter's settlement methodology with perfect consistency is worse than a human vaguely aware the rules changed, so rule maintenance needs a named owner and a change process tied to market notices.

Agents are also poor at genuine novelty. A new market mechanism, an unprecedented intervention, the first settlement run after a market suspension, these break the patterns the agent relies on, and it must be designed to say "this does not match anything I know" and stop, rather than produce a fluent, wrong reconciliation. Interpretation of ambiguous regulation stays with your compliance team and your lawyers; an agent can gather what a licence condition requires, but deciding what it means in a contested case is not extraction, it is judgement. And no agent should ever hold the submit button for a regulatory filing or a trade action; a named human does, always.

The pattern across all of it is consistent: the back office keeps its judgement and sheds its collation. Reconciliations run at full coverage instead of sampled coverage, deadlines stop being heroics, and every number can show its working.
