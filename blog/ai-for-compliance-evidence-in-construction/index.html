AI for compliance evidence in construction: proving what happened on site and when

1 October 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

Compliance evidence on a construction project is not produced inside a system of record. It is produced at the gate, on a scaffold, in a site cabin at the end of a wet shift, by people whose job is the work rather than the paperwork about the work. It arrives as a photograph of a card held up to a phone camera, a scan of a signed sheet with a thumb over one corner, a line in a site diary, and a messaging thread where a foreman confirms something important and then the thread scrolls away.

Then somebody asks a narrow question. Who lifted that beam, what were they qualified to do, what inspection was in force on the crane that day, who briefed the gang on the sequence. The answer usually exists. The cost is finding it, proving it belongs to that activity and not a similar one elsewhere on site, and being honest about what was never collected.

What the evidence actually is before anyone files it

The evidence is heterogeneous because it is created at the point of work by many different organisations. A single week on a medium site generates competence records and tickets with their own expiry dates, inspection records for plant and lifting accessories, method statements meant to describe today's activity, permits for hot works and excavation, briefing registers with signatures, environmental monitoring logs, material test certificates, temporary works designs with their permit to load, and a trail of design and change records that has to survive into the handover file. None of it sits in one place or one format.

Reading the intake and matching it to the obligation

This is the part AI is genuinely good at, and the part that consumes the most administrative time. An agent reads the raw intake for what it is: whose record it is, what it certifies, which plant item or activity it refers to, when it was issued and when it stops being valid. Then it matches that record to the obligation it should satisfy and files it against the right activity and location.

The useful output is not the filing. It is the register of what has no match. A certificate referring to plant not on your inventory. An obligation with nothing filed against it. Those cost money when an inspection finds them first.

Competence records and the expiry you did not see coming

The competence cards and tickets your operatives carry expire on their own schedule rather than yours, and nobody discovers that in advance by browsing a folder.

An agent keeps a live view per person and per organisation: what is held, what it covers, when it lapses, which inductions are complete. The cross-reference nobody does by hand is the valuable one. Work logged on a day when the ticket covering it had expired. A task carried out by someone whose card covers a related activity but not this one. Each flag should carry the record reference and the date, so a reviewer verifies rather than trusts.

Plant and lifting equipment, against the interval they are due on

Inspection records for plant, lifting equipment and accessories are only evidence if they cover the date the equipment was used and the configuration it was used in. In practice the record sits with the hire company, the usage sits in the site diary, and the two are joined only when challenged.

An agent links each item to the records held for it, confirms the statutory thorough examination and the periodic checks cover the period of use, then flags items in use with nothing on file, records expiring before a planned lift, and identifiers on the record that do not match the one visible on the item.

Method statements and risk assessments against the work in front of you

The honest failure mode on most sites is not a missing method statement. It is one that no longer describes what the gang is doing, because the sequence changed, the plant changed, or a temporary condition appeared that nobody assessed.

An agent compares the documents in force against what the site is recording: the diary, the permits issued, the briefings delivered. Where the activity in the daily record is not the activity the document covers, that is a flag worth having the same day. Whether the revised approach is safe is not its call.

Permits to work and the chain behind a single task

Permits are high consequence and often the least tidy evidence you hold, because they are produced at the point of work by people concentrating on the work.

An agent assembles the chain around one task: the permit issued, the conditions attached, the isolation it relied on, the people named, the close out. It checks that chain for internal consistency, and whether the work described matches what was authorised. Breaks go to the responsible person as a description of what is missing.

Daily briefings and the register that proves it happened

Briefing and toolbox talk records are the evidence most often collected and least often usable, because a signature sheet photographed on a clipboard is not searchable and nobody reconciles it against who was on site.

An agent reads the sheet, extracts the names and the subject, and reconciles it against the access records for that shift. The output is the gap: people on site for an activity with no briefing recorded, briefings on a topic that does not match the work, registers where the signatures cannot be read. Illegible is a real finding and should be reported as illegible rather than guessed.

Temporary works and the permit to load

Temporary works carry the sharpest consequence and the thinnest paper trail, because the sequence depends on a design, a check at the right category, a permit to load and a permit to dismantle.

An agent tracks each item through that sequence and reports two shapes of failure: items loaded with no recorded permit to load, and items whose design was revised without the check and permit being reissued against the revision. It does not assess the design.

Environmental monitoring and the conditions attached to the site

Monitoring obligations are periodic, tied to a location, and often discharged by third parties on their own schedules, so noise readings, discharge records and waste transfer documentation get reconciled, if at all, when somebody complains.

An agent reconciles what was collected against what the conditions attached to your site require at each location, flagging gaps while there is still time to act, and assembles the pack for a date and location when a complaint arrives. A named person decides whether something is notifiable.

The handover file as the end of a live trail

The handover file goes wrong because it is treated as a document produced at the end rather than the accumulated output of everything above. By the time anyone opens it, the people who could explain a gap have left and the subcontractor holding the missing certificate has been paid.

Treated as a live trail the position is different. The agent maintains the index from the start, keeps the link between a design change, the instruction behind it, what was built and the record proving it, and reports completeness continuously. The thread of design and change records that the building safety regime expects to survive into occupation is the thread that breaks quietly in flight. Checking it weekly is cheap; reconstructing it at completion is not.

Where AI fails here

It cannot tell you the site is safe

This is the hard line. Everything above is evidence handling: reading records, matching them to obligations, checking dates and chains, flagging absence. None of it is a judgement about whether the excavation is stable, the lift plan is sound, or the gang understood the briefing they signed. A complete set of paperwork over an unsafe operation is more dangerous than an incomplete set, because it removes the discomfort that makes people look.

So keep the agent on the question it can answer. Does the evidence exist, does it cover the right thing, is it current, what is missing. Whether the work is safe stays with the people accountable for it.

Failures rooted in the record

If the subcontractor register or the plant inventory is wrong, the agent will file good evidence confidently against the wrong entity, at scale, with a tidy citation trail. Neatness is persuasive. Register quality is the ceiling on all of this, which is why identity resolution must be allowed to return unresolved rather than pick the likeliest candidate.

Photographs mislead. An image can show plant correct in type and plausible in every respect while being the wrong unit. A legible plate is strong evidence and the agent should cite the visible text; a match resting only on time and location is weak and has to be labelled weak. Site shorthand is the other trap, because diary entries use local names only the team understands.

Failures rooted in how it is deployed

The worst outcome is an agent smoothing over a genuine gap. If a record does not exist, the honest output is that it does not exist, not an inference about what probably happened and not an adjacent record offered as the missing one.

The second worst is using the output as a performance measure on site teams. Flag counts will fall because the flags stop being raised, not because the evidence improved. If early output shows the work is not being recorded, that is the finding, not a paperwork problem to automate.

Proving it works before you rely on it

Do not evaluate this on a demo. Take inspections you have been through, where the questions and the packs produced are on record, and have the agent assemble those packs from scratch. Compare in both directions: what it missed, and what it found that the manual effort did not. Then take evidence files your own people consider good and files they consider poor, and check the agent rates them as your people do. An agent that calls a thin file complete has failed the only test that matters.

Begin where it only reads and the ground truth is checkable. Ticket and inspection expiry against logged work qualifies. Run it on one site before the portfolio, and bring the people who stand in front of an inspector into the design early.
