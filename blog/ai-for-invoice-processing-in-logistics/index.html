AI for Invoice Processing in Logistics

17 September 2026  ·  8 minute read  ·  Adopt AI Partners

An accounts payable team that has only ever handled supplier invoices will find the freight file baffling for a specific reason. There is usually no purchase order. Nobody raised a requisition, nobody approved a commitment, nothing was received into a warehouse against a line. A service was performed, often before anyone agreed what it would cost, and the bill is the carrier's own calculation of what their tariff says you owe.

So the question a freight invoice asks is not the one ordinary AP is built to answer. It is whether the carrier rated the shipment correctly against the agreement you signed, and whether the charges stacked on top of the linehaul happened and were your fault. That is freight bill audit: arithmetic against contract terms, at volume, under time pressure, that almost nobody completes today.

Matching to a shipment and a rate, not to a purchase order

The reference data for a freight invoice sits in your transport management system, not your ERP. Three things have to line up.

The shipment must exist. The invoice carries a reference of some kind: a consignment note number, a pro number, a booking, a container, a house bill, sometimes only a customer reference belonging to your own order. Resolving it to a real movement is harder than it sounds, because carriers rebill under corrected references and reuse formats across their operating companies.

The rate must be found. That means locating the contract, the lane, the service level, the effective date and the applicable tier, then recalculating the charge from scratch rather than accepting the figure on the page. Rates expire, carriers apply general increases from a date you may have negotiated around, and a table loaded six months ago may not be the one in force.

The event data must support the charges. Weight, dimensions, piece count, mileage, stops, wait times, dwell and delivery timestamps sit in your operational records and telematics, and they are your evidence against everything the carrier claims.

The agent then writes back a recalculated position per invoice: expected charge, billed charge, the difference, and the term or event that explains it. That output turns an audit from a sampling exercise into a complete one.

Accessorials are where the margin leaks

The linehaul is the easy part. It is usually right, because it is the number both sides looked at when the deal was done. The leakage is in accessorials: waiting time, liftgate, inside delivery, residential, redelivery, reconsignment, layover, hazardous handling, congestion, port charges, chassis, storage, quay rent, customs disbursement fees, cash advance fees on duty paid for you.

Each one is small, and each is a claim about something that happened in the physical world. Somebody waited. Somebody made a second attempt. A tail lift was needed.

So treat every accessorial as a claim requiring three tests. Is this charge code in the agreement at all, at what price, and is it capped. Does your own operational record show the triggering event. And is it consistent with the rest of the invoice, since a liftgate on a delivery to a distribution centre with a dock, or a residential surcharge on a commercial consignee, contradicts the rest of the movement.

Unnegotiated accessorials that quietly appear on invoices are among the highest yield findings here, and they are invisible at the line level. You see them by counting how often a code appears across a quarter.

Fuel surcharges

Fuel is a formula, which means it is auditable and almost never audited. The surcharge depends on a published index, the period that index is read from, the lag your contract specifies, the base price above which it applies, and whether it is charged as a percentage of linehaul or a rate per distance unit. It may or may not apply to accessorials, and that detail alone moves real money.

Recalculating it needs the index value for the correct period and the exact formula from your contract. It is mechanical and well suited to an agent, provided somebody has written the formula down in a form it can read. And a percentage surcharge applied to an overstated linehaul overcharges you twice.

Detention, demurrage and free time

These charges are a clock, and clocks are easy to get wrong in both directions.

Free time may run in calendar days or working days, may exclude weekends and public holidays, and it matters which country's holidays apply. Demurrage on the container at the terminal and detention on the equipment after it leaves are distinct charges from potentially distinct parties, and the handover between them is where double billing lives. The clock start may be discharge, availability, gate out or customs release, and those are not the same moment.

An agent recalculates elapsed time from your own gate and terminal events, applies the free time in the agreement, and states whether the days billed match the days incurred. Then it separates the second question, which is whose delay it was. A customs hold, a missing release, a full yard and a customer who would not take the container have different owners, and only one is a charge you should absorb. The agent assembles the timeline and names the gap. A person decides whether you pay, dispute or rebill it onward.

Rated versus quoted, and the reweigh

A quote is not a rating. Someone quoted a price from declared weights, dimensions and a class. The carrier then weighed and measured the freight itself and rated what it found.

Where the two differ you have three problems that get muddled into one. The carrier may have rated wrongly. The declared data may have been wrong, in which case the invoice is right and your quote was not. Or the freight genuinely changed. Reweighs and reclassifications need the carrier's inspection evidence pulled and compared against your packing data, and an agent should present that comparison rather than deciding it. What it can decide is arithmetic: whether the rating logic was applied correctly given the figures the carrier used.

The useful output is the pattern rather than the credit. When one shipper's declared dimensions are short every week, the fix sits upstream of accounts payable entirely.

Multi leg movements billed by several parties

One door to door movement produces invoices from a road haulier, an ocean or air carrier, a terminal, a customs broker, a forwarder rebilling some of the same legs with a margin, and a last mile operator. Each invoice is correct in isolation. The risk is in the overlap.

So assemble all charges against a single movement identity rather than auditing invoices one at a time, and look for the failures that only appear at that level: the same accessorial billed by both the forwarder and the underlying carrier, a disbursement fee charged on duty that also carried a percentage handling fee, a subcontracted leg appearing twice under two references, and pass through costs rebilled above what the underlying invoice said. Where you hold that invoice, the last check is straightforward and rarely performed.

The movement level view is also the only reliable duplicate test in freight. A corrected invoice, a supplemental for a late accessorial and a balance due bill are all legitimate, while the real duplicate often arrives under a different invoice number entirely. So match on charge code, date and amount against everything ever billed for that movement rather than on the invoice header.

Short pays, chargebacks and disputes that never close

Freight disputes are not settled like supplier disputes, because the convention is that you pay the part you accept and short pay the rest. That creates its own mess. The withheld balance sits open on the carrier's ledger, reappears weeks later as a balance due invoice under a new number, and gets paid by somebody who cannot see it was withheld deliberately.

An agent is useful here mainly as memory. It records the disputed amount, the reason code, the evidence and the date raised, links all of it to the movement, then recognises the rebill when it arrives rather than treating it as a new charge. It chases the carrier on a schedule, drafts the dispute for a person to send, and tracks the credit note back to the original line so the matter closes rather than being forgotten.

Ageing matters. Most carrier agreements give you a limited window to raise a billing dispute, so an unworked exception queue silently becomes acceptance of the charge.

Where AI fails here

Contracts that live in a spreadsheet nobody maintains defeat the whole exercise. If the rate tables, accessorial schedules, free time terms and fuel formula are not held anywhere current and machine readable, the agent has nothing to audit against and will validate charges using stale terms. Most of the work in these projects is getting agreement data into shape, which is worth saying before a deployment rather than during it.

Missing operational evidence caps what is provable. If your own gate times, wait times and delivery timestamps are not captured, you cannot disprove a detention charge, and saying it is unevidenced is a weaker position than showing it is wrong. Cause is worse still. Establishing that a delay was the customer's, the terminal's or your own is an inference from an incomplete record, and an agent that asserts fault will sometimes be wrong in a way that costs you a relationship.

A recalculated figure can be confidently incorrect. The failure mode is a clean audit result derived from the wrong rate tier or effective date, and because it reads precisely nobody rechecks it. Keep the contract clause and the source event visible next to every variance raised.

Commercial judgement stays human. Whether to dispute a modest overcharge with a carrier you depend on during a capacity crunch is a relationship decision, not an arithmetic one, as is deciding which overcharges are rebillable to your customer. The agent finds the money. Someone else decides whether to go and get it.

Where to start

Run it backwards first. Take a quarter of paid freight invoices where the money is already out the door, and have the agent audit them against the contracts in force at the time. You risk nothing, and the recovery it finds pays for the exercise while telling you which failure modes are yours.

Then measure two things separately. Recovery value tells you whether the audit works. Exception volume tells you whether your operation is sound, and a falling exception rate on a carrier is worth more than a rising recovery figure.
